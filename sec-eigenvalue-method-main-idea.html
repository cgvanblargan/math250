    Section 5.5.1 The Main Idea of the Eigenvalue Method

    Recall from Definition 5.2.1 that a first-order linear system has the form
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= p_{11}(t)x_1 + p_{12}(t)x_2 + f_1(t)\\ x_2' \amp= p_{21}(t)x_1 + p_{22}(t)x_2 + f_2(t), \end{align*}
    which we can write more compactly as the matrix equation \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{f}(t)\) (see Fact 5.2.2).
    🔗
    In this section, we focus on the case where all the coefficients \(p_{ij}\) are constants, and the system is homogeneous, meaning \(\mathbf{f}(t) = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) That is, we will solve systems of the form:
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= ax_1 + bx_2\\ x_2' \amp= cx_1 + dx_2, \end{align*}
    or in matrix form,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}, \qquad \text{where } A = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix} \text{ is a constant matrix.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let’s explore how we might try to solve \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}\text{.}\) Let’s think about what happened with just a single function. The equation
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = ky \end{equation*}
    has solutions \(y(t) = Ce^{kt}\text{.}\) So for our system, which has a similar structure (the derivative equals constant times function), it’s natural to guess that the solution is “something with an \(e\) in it.” Let’s try:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}, \end{equation*}
    where \(\mathbf{v}\) is a constant vector and \(\lambda\) is a constant number. Written out, this guess is:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 e^{\lambda t} \\ v_2 e^{\lambda t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It turns out that this guess works, as long as \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\lambda\) are an eigenvector-eigenvalue pair for \(A\text{!}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 5.5.1. Eigenvector Solutions.

    Suppose \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) with associated eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t} \end{equation*}
    is a solution of \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Justification on why \(\mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}\) is a solution.

    We plug the guess \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}\) into both sides of \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}\) and check that they match.
    🔗
    For the left-hand side, since \(\mathbf{v}\) is a constant vector, differentiating each component brings down a factor of \(\lambda\) from the chain rule:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 e^{\lambda t} \\ v_2 e^{\lambda t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda v_1 e^{\lambda t} \\ \lambda v_2 e^{\lambda t} \end{bmatrix} = \lambda\mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For the right-hand side, since \(e^{\lambda t}\) is a scalar, we can pull it out of the matrix product, and then use the eigenvector property \(A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{x} = A\left(\mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}\right) = \left(A\mathbf{v}\right)e^{\lambda t} = \lambda\mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Both sides came out to \(\lambda\mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}\) satisfies the equation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s put this fact together with our work from the last section. There, we found the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1 = -2 \text{ with } \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_2 = 5 \text{ with } \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Activity 5.5.1. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Go Together.

    Using the eigenvalues and eigenvectors above, which of the following are solutions of \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\text{?}\) Select all that apply.
    🔗
    • \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}e^{-2t}\)
    • Correct! The eigenvector \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\) is paired with its own eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 = -2\text{,}\) so Theorem 5.5.1 says this is a solution.
    • \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{5t}\)
    • Correct! The eigenvector \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is paired with its own eigenvalue \(\lambda_2 = 5\text{,}\) so Theorem 5.5.1 says this is a solution.
    • \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}e^{5t}\)
    • Careful: the eigenvector and the eigenvalue must go together. The eigenvector \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\) belongs to \(\lambda_1 = -2\text{,}\) not to \(5\text{.}\) Pairing it with the wrong eigenvalue does not give a solution.
    • \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-2t}\)
    • Careful: the eigenvector and the eigenvalue must go together. The eigenvector \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) belongs to \(\lambda_2 = 5\text{,}\) not to \(-2\text{.}\) Pairing it with the wrong eigenvalue does not give a solution.
    🔗
    So each eigenvalue-eigenvector pair yields a solution. Recall that the eigenvalues come from solving
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - \lambda I) = 0, \end{equation*}
    which is called the characteristic equation of \(A\text{.}\) For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, the characteristic equation is a quadratic in \(\lambda\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0. \end{equation*}
    From our experience with quadratics (remember the second-order ODEs in Chapter 3!), there are three possibilities for the roots.
    🔗

    Observation 5.5.2. The Three Cases of Eigenvalues for \(2 \times 2\) Matrices.

    The eigenvalues of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(A\) fall into one of three cases:
    1. two distinct real eigenvalues,
      🔗
      🔗
    2. two complex conjugate eigenvalues,
      🔗
      🔗
    3. one repeated real eigenvalue.
      🔗
      🔗
    Each of these three cases corresponds to a strategy used to solve \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In this section, we will cover the strategies for the first two cases: distinct real eigenvalues and complex conjugate eigenvalues. We start with the case of two distinct real eigenvalues on the next page.
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • The systems we are solving: first-order, homogeneous, linear systems with constant coefficients, \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) where \(A\) is a constant matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The main idea: if \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) with eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) then \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{\lambda t}\) is a solution. This is why we learned to find eigenvalues and eigenvectors!
      🔗
      🔗
    • Three cases: the characteristic equation \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) is a quadratic, so the eigenvalues are either distinct real, complex conjugates, or one repeated real number. Each case has its own solution strategy.
      🔗
      🔗
