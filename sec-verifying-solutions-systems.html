    Section 5.2.2 Verifying Solutions To a System

    Back in the very first chapter, before we could solve ODEs, we learned how to verify that a given function was a solution: plug it in and check that both sides match. We’ll take the same first step with systems. Given a vector of functions, how do we check whether it solves the matrix equation \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{f}(t)\text{?}\) As before, we just compute both sides and compare!
    🔗
    One note on notation regarding the next example: we’ll name our candidate solutions \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) so their names are distinct from the component functions \(x_1\) and \(x_2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 5.2.1. Verifying Solutions.

    Consider the system:
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= 4x_1 - 3x_2\\ x_2' \amp= 6x_1 - 7x_2 \end{align*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Write the system as a matrix equation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every coefficient is present here (no missing terms!), and there are no leftover terms, so \(\mathbf{f}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 6 \amp -7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\mathbf{f}(t)\) is the zero vector, we usually just drop it and write
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 6 \amp -7 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Verify that \(\mathbf{y}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix}\) is a solution.
    🔗
    Let’s do this one together. We need to check that \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{y}}{dt} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{y}\text{,}\) so we compute each side separately.
    🔗
    For the left-hand side, we differentiate each component:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{y}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{d}{dt}\left(3e^{2t}\right) \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(2e^{2t}\right) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6e^{2t} \\ 4e^{2t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For the right-hand side, we multiply:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{P}\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 6 \amp -7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} \amp= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \cdot 3e^{2t} - 3 \cdot 2e^{2t} \\ 6 \cdot 3e^{2t} - 7 \cdot 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{bmatrix} (12 - 6)e^{2t} \\ (18 - 14)e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6e^{2t} \\ 4e^{2t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    The two sides match, so \(\mathbf{y}(t)\) is a solution!
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now it’s your turn: verify that \(\mathbf{z}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\) is also a solution. Work it out on paper before checking the solution below, and then use the checkpoint question that follows to confirm your computation.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Follow the same two steps: differentiate each component of \(\mathbf{z}\) to get \(\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt}\) (don’t forget the chain rule!), then compute \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{z}\) with matrix-vector multiplication. You’re hoping the two results agree.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Differentiating (chain rule brings down a \(-5\)):
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} -5e^{-5t} \\ -15e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{P}\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 6 \amp -7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} \amp= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \cdot e^{-5t} - 3 \cdot 3e^{-5t} \\ 6 \cdot e^{-5t} - 7 \cdot 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{bmatrix} (4-9)e^{-5t} \\ (6-21)e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5e^{-5t} \\ -15e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    Both sides equal \(\begin{bmatrix} -5e^{-5t} \\ -15e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{z}(t)\) is a solution as well.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 5.2.1. Checking Your Verification.

    When verifying \(\mathbf{z}(t)\) in the last part, you computed both \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt}\) and \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{z}\text{.}\) What vector did you get (for both)?
    🔗
    • \(\begin{bmatrix} -5e^{-5t} \\ -15e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Correct! The chain rule brings a factor of \(-5\) out of each component of \(\mathbf{z}\text{,}\) and the matrix product gives \((4-9)e^{-5t} = -5e^{-5t}\) and \((6-21)e^{-5t} = -15e^{-5t}\text{.}\) The two sides agree, so \(\mathbf{z}(t)\) is a solution.
    • \(\begin{bmatrix} 5e^{-5t} \\ 15e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Check your signs! Differentiating \(e^{-5t}\) brings down a factor of \(-5\text{,}\) not \(5\text{.}\) And on the matrix side, \(4 - 9 = -5\) and \(6 - 21 = -15\text{:}\) both negative.
    • \(\begin{bmatrix} -e^{-5t} \\ -3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\)
    • This is just \(-\mathbf{z}(t)\text{,}\) which suggests the chain rule got skipped. Differentiating \(e^{-5t}\) brings down the full factor of \(-5\text{:}\) \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-5t} = -5e^{-5t}\text{.}\)
    • The two sides did not match, so \(\mathbf{z}(t)\) is not a solution.
    • They should match! Recompute carefully: the derivative side gives \(-5e^{-5t}\) and \(-15e^{-5t}\text{,}\) and the matrix side gives \((4-9)e^{-5t}\) and \((6-21)e^{-5t}\text{,}\) which are the same thing. A stray sign or a slipped chain rule is the usual culprit here.
    🔗
    We just found two different solutions to the same system. There are actually infinitely many more! Just like with second-order equations back in Chapter 3, we’ll soon see how to combine solutions to build the general solution.
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • To verify that a vector of functions solves \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) compute both sides separately and check that they give the same vector.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The left-hand side is computed by differentiating each component (watch the chain rule!), and the right-hand side by matrix-vector multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A system can have several different solutions. Soon we will see how to combine them into the general solution.
