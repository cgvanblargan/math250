    Section 3.2.2 Period and Frequency

    From the previous page, we already know how to set up and solve the undamped mass-spring system. As a reminder, when \(m\) is the mass and \(k\) is the spring constant of the system, the position function \(x(t)\) satisfies the ODE
    \begin{equation*} mx'' + kx = 0, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad x'' + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \end{equation*}
    where \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\text{.}\) And the solution is
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t), \end{equation*}
    which is called simple harmonic motion
    🔗
    Now at this point, it may feel like we have a full understanding of this ODE model. However, just solving for \(x(t)\) does not give us the full picture. Often in applications, we want to take this a step further and really understand the behavior of the solutions. A big theme from here on is exploring how the mathematics relates to the physical behavior of the spring.
    🔗
    We begin with a quick example to make sure we understand how to solve the ODE. Then we will explore some more properties.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.1. A 10 kg Mass on a Spring.

    A block of mass \(m = 10\) kg is attached to a spring with spring constant \(k = 140\) N/m. Find the general solution \(x(t)\) describing its position.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Plug \(m = 10\) and \(k = 140\) into \(mx'' + kx = 0\) and solve.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Substituting \(m = 10\) and \(k = 140\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 10x'' + 140x = 0. \end{equation*}
    Dividing through by 10:
    \begin{equation*} x'' + 14x = 0. \end{equation*}
    So \(\omega_0^2 = 14\text{,}\) giving \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{14}\text{.}\) The general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = c_1\cos\!\left(\sqrt{14}\, t\right) + c_2\sin\!\left(\sqrt{14}\, t\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As we said at the beginning of this page, we don’t just care about the equation \(x(t) = c_1\cos\!\left(\sqrt{14}\, t\right) + c_2\sin\!\left(\sqrt{14}\, t\right).\) We want to understand what is actually happening in the mass-spring system. In particular, we care about the period of the solution: the time it takes to complete one full oscillation. As you play with the interactive below, pay attention to what happens as you change \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{:}\) does the period change? Also notice how the spring animation on the left relates to the graph on the right.
    🔗
    Notice that no matter what you set \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) to, the shaded region (one full oscillation) always has the same width. The shape of the curve changes, but the time it takes to complete one cycle doesn’t. That time is what we call the \(period\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What does affect the period is the natural frequency of the system: \(\omega_0\text{.}\) In the graph below, observe what happens to a cosine function as you change the value of \(\omega_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As \(\omega_0\) increases, the position function \(x(t)=2\cos(\omega_0 t)\) oscillates faster, so the period decreases. We know that \(A\cos(t)\) has a period of \(2\pi\text{.}\) If you make it move twice as fast, as in \(A\cos(2t)\text{,}\) then the period is cut in half: \(\frac{2\pi}{2}=\pi\text{.}\) In general, scaling time by a factor of \(\omega_0\) gives a period of:
    \begin{equation*} \text{period} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A related quantity is frequency: how many full oscillations occur per unit time. Bigger \(\omega_0\) means faster oscillation and therefore higher frequency. In fact, frequency is just the reciprocal of the period.
    🔗
    Let’s summarize this below.
    🔗

    Observation 3.2.4. Period and Frequency of Simple Harmonic Motion.

    For simple harmonic motion \(x(t) = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)\text{:}\)
    • The period (time to complete one full oscillation) is:
      \begin{equation*} T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • The frequency (number of oscillations per unit time) is:
      \begin{equation*} f = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{T}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    Larger \(\omega_0\) means faster oscillation: shorter period, higher frequency.
    🔗
    🔗
    Now for a quick check!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.5.

    For the 10 kg mass-spring system from Example 3.2.1, where \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{14}\text{,}\) which of the following correctly gives the period \(T\) and frequency \(f\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{14}}\) and \(f = \dfrac{\sqrt{14}}{2\pi}\)
    • Exactly right. With \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{14}\text{,}\) the period is \(T = 2\pi/\sqrt{14} \approx 1.68\) seconds, and the frequency is \(f = \sqrt{14}/(2\pi) \approx 0.60\) oscillations per second. \(\checkmark\)
    • \(T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{14}}\) and \(f = \dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{14}}\)
    • The period is correct, but frequency and period are reciprocals of each other, not equal. Frequency is \(f = 1/T = \sqrt{14}/(2\pi)\text{.}\)
    • \(T = \sqrt{14}\) and \(f = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{14}}\)
    • The period formula is \(T = 2\pi/\omega_0\text{,}\) not just \(\omega_0\) itself. The \(2\pi\) comes from the fact that cosine completes one full cycle over an interval of length \(2\pi\text{.}\)
    • \(T = \dfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{14}}\) and \(f = \dfrac{\sqrt{14}}{\pi}\)
    • Close, but off by a factor of 2. One full oscillation of cosine spans \(2\pi\text{,}\) not \(\pi\text{,}\) that would only be a half-cycle. The correct period is \(T = 2\pi/\sqrt{14}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Period: \(T = 2\pi/\omega_0\text{,}\) the time for one complete oscillation. Depends only on \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\text{,}\) not on how far you pull the mass.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Frequency: \(f = \omega_0/(2\pi) = 1/T\text{,}\) oscillations per unit time. Larger \(\omega_0\) means higher frequency and shorter period.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Initial conditions don’t affect period: changing \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) changes the shape of the oscillation (amplitude, starting position) but not how fast it oscillates.
      🔗
      🔗
