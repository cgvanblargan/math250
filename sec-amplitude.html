    Section 3.2.4 Distance from Equilibrium

    We are not quite done exploring the behavior of solutions to the free undamped model! We know the mass oscillates forever. In this page we explore how far from equilibrium it actually gets. Watch the spring below, and pay attention to when it’s at its most stretched and most compressed! How does this connect to what’s happening in the graph on the right?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.2.

    From the graph above, what is the furthest distance the mass ever gets from equilibrium?
    🔗
    • \(2\)
    • Right: the graph peaks at \(x = 2\) and bottoms out at \(x = -2\text{,}\) so the mass never gets more than 2 units from equilibrium. \(\checkmark\)
    • \(3\)
    • The 3 is \(\omega_0\text{,}\) the frequency: it controls how fast the mass oscillates, not how far it goes. Look at the graph: the curve never reaches \(y = 3\text{.}\)
    • \(4\)
    • The mass swings between \(x = -2\) and \(x = 2\text{,}\) so the total distance from one extreme to the other is 4, but the question asks for distance from equilibrium, which is just half of that.
    • \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\)
    • That’s the period of \(x(t) = 2\cos(3t)\) (\(T = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2\pi/3\)), a time, not a distance. Look at the height of the curve on the graph for the maximum distance.
    🔗
    That maximum distance is just the amplitude of the cosine function, which equals the coefficient out front. In general, for any solution of the form \(x(t) = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)\text{,}\) the amplitude is \(A = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Observation 3.2.3. Maximum Distance from Equilibrium.

    If \(x(t) = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)\) is the position of a mass in a spring-mass system, then the maximum distance the mass ever gets from equilibrium is the amplitude:
    \begin{equation*} A = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s put all of this together with an example. We will be exploring an additional question at the end regarding the speed of the spring.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.4. A Mass-Spring System: Amplitude and Speed.

    Suppose a mass-spring system is described by
    \begin{equation*} y'' + 4y = 0, \qquad y(0) = -3, \quad y'(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a) Step 1: Solve the IVP.

    Find \(y(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    This is in the form of Observation 3.1.2: identify \(\omega\) and write down the general solution, then apply the initial conditions.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The equation \(y'' + 4y = 0\) is in the form of Observation 3.1.2 with \(\omega = 2\text{,}\) so the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = c_1\cos(2t) + c_2\sin(2t). \end{equation*}
    Applying initial conditions:
    \begin{align*} y(0) = c_1 \amp= -3,\\ y'(0) = 2c_2 \amp= 0 \implies c_2 = 0. \end{align*}
    So the solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = -3\cos(2t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Step 2: How far from equilibrium?

    What is the furthest the mass gets from equilibrium?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Identify the amplitude \(A = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(c_1 = -3\) and \(c_2 = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 0^2} = 3. \end{equation*}
    The mass gets at most \(3\) units from equilibrium. We can also see this directly: \(y(t) = -3\cos(2t)\) has maximum value 3 and minimum value \(-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Step 3: Does it slow down over time?

    Will the mass move slower as it passes through equilibrium the 5th time compared to the 1st time?
    🔗
    Hint.
    1. Compute \(y'(t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find all times \(t^*\) when the mass passes through equilibrium, i.e. solve \(y(t^*) = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Evaluate the speed \(|y'(t^*)|\) at those times and compare.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Compute \(y'(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y'(t) = 6\sin(2t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    2. The mass passes through equilibrium when \(y(t^*) = 0\text{,}\) i.e. \(-3\cos(2t^*) = 0\text{,}\) so \(\cos(2t^*) = 0\text{,}\) giving:
    \begin{equation*} t^* = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{n\pi}{2}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    3. Evaluate the speed at each crossing:
    \begin{equation*} |y'(t^*)| = |6\sin(2t^*)| = \left|6\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi\right)\right|. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\sin(\pi/2 + n\pi) = \pm 1\) for all integers \(n\text{,}\) we get:
    \begin{equation*} |y'(t^*)| = 6 \quad \text{every time.} \end{equation*}
    The speed at equilibrium is always exactly 6, whether it’s the 1st crossing or the 5th. Free undamped motion has constant amplitude: no energy is lost, so the mass moves just as fast through equilibrium every time.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So there we have it. From the previous pages, we learned how to interpret and determine the frequency and period of mass. Then we explored how it goes back and forth through the equilibrium position infinitely many times as time goes on forever. And now, we understand how to connect the amplitude of \(x(t)\) with the distance the spring is from the equilibrium. We also explored the slightly more subtle point that the speed at which it passes through equilibrium never changes. This should be clear from the graph though, as \(x(t)\) is a periodic function that keeps repeating the same motion forever!
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Amplitude: the maximum distance from equilibrium is \(A = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}\text{.}\) For a pure cosine or sine solution, it’s just the coefficient out front.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Speed at equilibrium is constant: since there’s no damping, no energy is lost. The mass passes through equilibrium at the same speed every time.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Free undamped motion is perfectly periodic: same amplitude, same speed, same period, forever! Damping changes all of this, as we’ll see next.
