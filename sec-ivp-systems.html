    Section 5.3.3 Initial Value Problems for Systems

    We now know how to find the general solution to a homogeneous system. As always, the general solution gives every possible solution. But in applications (like our rabbits and foxes), we usually know where the system starts, and we want the one specific solution solving the initial value problem.
    🔗

    Observation 5.3.1.

    The general solution \(c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z}\) has two unknown constants. So if we’re given two initial conditions, the values of \(x_1(0)\) and \(x_2(0)\text{,}\) we can solve for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) and find the one particular solution that satisfies them. This is just like second-order equations, where the two constants in the general solution required two initial conditions.
    🔗
    (It turns out that linear independence of the solutions \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) guarantees there is a unique solution for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s the system we’ll work with on this page. Before the new material, let’s redo the process from the last page on it. For the system
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \amp -2 \\ 12 \amp -7 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}, \end{equation*}
    one can show that
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{y}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} e^{5t} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{z}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \end{equation*}
    are both solutions. (You could verify this yourself with the process from Section 5.2.2!)
    🔗
    In the following activity, we repeat the same process covered in the previous two pages. Feel free to go through it, skim, or skip the warm-up depending on how much practice you want with it!
    🔗

    Activity 5.3.1. Warm-Up: Find the General Solution.

    (a)

    Compute the Wronskian to show \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) are linearly independent.
    🔗
    Hint.
    The solutions become the columns:
    \begin{equation*} W = \det\left( \begin{bmatrix} e^{5t} \amp e^{-5t} \\ e^{5t} \amp 6e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} \right), \qquad \det \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Computing:
    \begin{equation*} W = e^{5t} \cdot 6e^{-5t} - e^{-5t} \cdot e^{5t} = 6e^{0} - e^{0} = 6 - 1 = 5. \end{equation*}
    Since \(W = 5 \neq 0\text{,}\) the solutions are linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Write the general solution, combining it into a single vector.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Since \(W \neq 0\text{,}\) the general solution is \(c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z}\text{.}\) Distribute the constants and add component-wise to get one vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} e^{5t} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} e^{-5t} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 e^{5t} + c_2 e^{-5t} \\ c_1 e^{5t} + 6c_2 e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now it is time for the main example of this page. We will use initial conditions to find the particular solution.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.2. Finding a Particular Solution.

    For the system above, find the particular solution where \(x_1(0) = 7\) and \(x_2(0) = -3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the warm-up (where we already checked \(W \neq 0\text{!}\)), the general solution is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 e^{5t} + c_2 e^{-5t} \\ c_1 e^{5t} + 6c_2 e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    By plugging in \(t = 0\) and using the initial conditions, we get the system:
    \begin{align*} 7 \amp= c_1 + c_2\\ -3 \amp= c_1 + 6c_2 \end{align*}
    Solution 1. Work for getting the system (read if stuck)
    Plugging \(t = 0\) into the first component of the general solution:
    \begin{equation*} x_1(0) = c_1 e^{5 \cdot 0} + c_2 e^{-5 \cdot 0} = c_1 e^{0} + c_2 e^{0} = c_1 + c_2, \end{equation*}
    since \(e^{0} = 1\text{.}\) Setting this equal to the initial condition \(x_1(0) = 7\) gives \(7 = c_1 + c_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Similarly, for the second component:
    \begin{equation*} x_2(0) = c_1 e^{5 \cdot 0} + 6c_2 e^{-5 \cdot 0} = c_1 e^{0} + 6c_2 e^{0} = c_1 + 6c_2, \end{equation*}
    and setting this equal to \(x_2(0) = -3\) gives \(-3 = c_1 + 6c_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This is two equations in the two unknowns \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{.}\) Solving the system gives:
    \begin{equation*} c_1 = 9, \qquad c_2 = -2. \end{equation*}
    Solution 2. Algebra (can skim)
    We solve the system
    \begin{align*} 7 \amp= c_1 + c_2\\ -3 \amp= c_1 + 6c_2 \end{align*}
    by elimination. Subtracting the second equation from the first cancels the \(c_1\)’s:
    \begin{equation*} 7 - (-3) = (c_1 + c_2) - (c_1 + 6c_2) \implies 10 = -5c_2 \implies c_2 = -2. \end{equation*}
    Then substituting \(c_2 = -2\) into the first equation:
    \begin{equation*} 7 = c_1 + (-2) \implies c_1 = 9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Substituting these back into the general solution, the particular solution is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 9 e^{5t} - 2 e^{-5t} \\ 9 e^{5t} - 12 e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    or written out by components, \(x_1(t) = 9e^{5t} - 2e^{-5t}\) and \(x_2(t) = 9e^{5t} - 12e^{-5t}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We end with a quick checkpoint:
    🔗

    Activity 5.3.2. Setting Up the Constants.

    Suppose a system has general solution
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 e^{3t} + 2c_2 e^{-t} \\ c_1 e^{3t} + 5c_2 e^{-t} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    with initial conditions \(x_1(0) = 4\) and \(x_2(0) = 1\text{.}\) What system of equations do we get for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{?}\) (You don’t need to solve it!)
    🔗
    • \(4 = c_1 + 2c_2\) and \(1 = c_1 + 5c_2\)
    • Correct! Plugging \(t = 0\) into the general solution makes every exponential equal \(e^{0} = 1\text{,}\) so the first component gives \(4 = c_1 + 2c_2\) and the second gives \(1 = c_1 + 5c_2\text{.}\) From here it’s just algebra to find \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{.}\)
    • \(4 = c_1 e^{3t} + 2c_2 e^{-t}\) and \(1 = c_1 e^{3t} + 5c_2 e^{-t}\)
    • Almost, but the initial conditions happen at the specific time \(t = 0\text{,}\) so we plug in \(t = 0\) everywhere. Since \(e^{0} = 1\text{,}\) the exponentials all disappear, leaving equations with only the constants: \(4 = c_1 + 2c_2\) and \(1 = c_1 + 5c_2\text{.}\)
    • \(1 = c_1 + 2c_2\) and \(4 = c_1 + 5c_2\)
    • Watch which condition goes with which component! \(x_1(0) = 4\) pairs with the first component of the general solution, giving \(4 = c_1 + 2c_2\text{,}\) and \(x_2(0) = 1\) pairs with the second, giving \(1 = c_1 + 5c_2\text{.}\) Here they’ve been swapped.
    • \(4 = 3c_1 + 2c_2\) and \(1 = 3c_1 + 5c_2\)
    • It looks like the exponentials got differentiated: the \(3\) from \(e^{3t}\) only comes down if we take a derivative, and initial conditions here are on \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) themselves, not their derivatives. Plugging \(t = 0\) straight into the general solution gives \(e^{0} = 1\) everywhere: \(4 = c_1 + 2c_2\) and \(1 = c_1 + 5c_2\text{.}\)
