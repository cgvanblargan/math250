    Section 3.6.2 Two Examples of the Forced System

    We now solve the forced undamped system for two different values of the spring constant \(k\text{.}\) The method is undetermined coefficients, which you practiced in Section 3.5.3 and surrounding sections, so feel free to skim the algebraic details in parts (b) and (c). What matters most is understanding which case you are in and what the general solution looks like at the end.
    🔗
    Throughout both examples we use \(m = 1\text{,}\) \(F_0 = 50\text{,}\) \(G_0 = 0\text{,}\) and \(\omega = 3\text{,}\) so the ODE is
    \begin{equation*} x'' + kx = 50\cos(3t), \end{equation*}
    and we find the general solution for two choices of \(k\text{:}\) first \(k = 8\text{,}\) then \(k = 9\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Activity 3.6.1. Solving the Forced System for Two Values of \(k\).

    In both examples below, the ODE is
    \begin{equation*} x'' + kx = 50\cos(3t), \end{equation*}
    and we find the general solution for two choices of \(k\text{:}\) \(k = 8\) and \(k = 9\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a) Part (a): Which example is the case of duplication?

    Which of the two examples is in the case of duplication? (See Theorem 3.5.2 for a reminder.)
    🔗
    • The example with \(k = 9\text{,}\) because the homogeneous equation becomes \(x'' + 9x = 0\text{,}\) i.e. \(x'' + 3^2 x = 0\text{,}\) so \(x_c\) contains \(\cos(3t)\) and \(\sin(3t)\text{.}\) The standard guess \(A\cos(3t) + B\sin(3t)\) already solves the homogeneous equation, giving us the case of duplication.
    • Correct! When \(k = 9\) the homogeneous equation is \(x'' + 3^2 x = 0\text{,}\) so \(x_c\) already contains \(\cos(3t)\) and \(\sin(3t)\text{.}\) The standard guess duplicates \(x_c\) and we must multiply by \(t\text{.}\) Note that this happened because \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{9} = 3 = \omega\text{:}\) the natural frequency equals the forcing frequency. \(\checkmark\)
    • The example with \(k = 8\text{,}\) because \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{8} \neq 3\) makes the algebra messier.
    • Messier algebra does not mean duplication. The case of duplication is about whether the guess for \(x_p\) already appears in \(x_c\text{,}\) which depends on whether \(\omega_0 = \omega\text{,}\) not on how clean the numbers are.
    • Both examples are cases of duplication, since both have cosine on the right-hand side.
    • Having cosine on the right-hand side is not enough to cause duplication. Duplication happens only when the frequency of that cosine matches \(\omega_0\text{.}\) That only occurs when \(k = 9\text{.}\)
    • Neither example is a case of duplication.
    • When \(k = 9\text{,}\) the natural frequency is \(\omega_0 = 3\text{,}\) which equals the forcing frequency \(\omega = 3\text{.}\) So \(\cos(3t)\) and \(\sin(3t)\) already appear in \(x_c\text{,}\) and we do have duplication.
    🔗

    (b) Part (b): General solution when \(k = 8\).

    Find the general solution to \(x'' + 8x = 50\cos(3t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Complementary solution:
    \begin{equation*} x_c = c_1\cos(\sqrt{8}\,t) + c_2\sin(\sqrt{8}\,t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Particular solution:
    \begin{equation*} x_p = -50\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    General solution:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = c_1\cos(\sqrt{8}\,t) + c_2\sin(\sqrt{8}\,t) - 50\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution. Finding \(x_p\) (can skim)
    Since \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{8} \neq 3 = \omega\text{,}\) this is the normal case. We guess \(x_p = A\cos(3t) + B\sin(3t)\) and compute:
    \begin{align*} x_p' \amp= -3A\sin(3t) + 3B\cos(3t)\\ x_p'' \amp= -9A\cos(3t) - 9B\sin(3t) \end{align*}
    Substituting into \(x'' + 8x\) and collecting:
    \begin{equation*} -A\cos(3t) - B\sin(3t) = 50\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution. Matching coefficients (can skim)
    \begin{align*} -A \amp= 50 \implies A = -50\\ -B \amp= 0 \implies B = 0 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    So \(x_p = -50\cos(3t)\text{.}\) Note that \(B = 0\) because there is no \(x'\) term in the ODE and the right-hand side has no sine.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Part (c): General solution when \(k = 9\).

    Find the general solution to \(x'' + 9x = 50\cos(3t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Complementary solution:
    \begin{equation*} x_c = c_1\cos(3t) + c_2\sin(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Particular solution:
    \begin{equation*} x_p = \frac{50}{6}\,t\sin(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    General solution:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = c_1\cos(3t) + c_2\sin(3t) + \frac{50}{6}\,t\sin(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution. Finding \(x_p\) (can skim)
    Since \(\omega_0 = 3 = \omega\text{,}\) we have duplication. The modified guess is \(x_p = t\bigl(A\cos(3t) + B\sin(3t)\bigr)\text{.}\) Computing derivatives by the product rule:
    \begin{align*} x_p' \amp= \bigl(A\cos(3t) + B\sin(3t)\bigr) + t\bigl(-3A\sin(3t) + 3B\cos(3t)\bigr)\\ x_p'' \amp= -6A\sin(3t) + 6B\cos(3t) + t\bigl(-9A\cos(3t) - 9B\sin(3t)\bigr) \end{align*}
    Substituting into \(x'' + 9x\text{,}\) the \(t\) terms cancel and we get:
    \begin{equation*} -6A\sin(3t) + 6B\cos(3t) = 50\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution. Matching coefficients (can skim)
    \begin{align*} 6B \amp= 50 \implies B = \tfrac{50}{6}\\ -6A \amp= 0 \implies A = 0 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    So \(x_p = \tfrac{50}{6}\,t\sin(3t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Look at the two general solutions side by side:
    \begin{align*} k = 8: \quad x(t) \amp= c_1\cos(\sqrt{8}\,t) + c_2\sin(\sqrt{8}\,t) - 50\cos(3t)\\ k = 9: \quad x(t) \amp= c_1\cos(3t) + c_2\sin(3t) + \tfrac{50}{6}\,t\sin(3t) \end{align*}
    They look quite different. What was it about the setup that caused this?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.6.1.

    What is fundamentally different between the two examples that led to such different solutions?
    🔗
    • In the first example \(\omega = 3\) and \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{8}\) are different, so we get a standard particular solution. In the second example \(\omega = \omega_0 = 3\text{,}\) which puts us in the case of duplication and introduces the factor of \(t\) in \(x_p\text{.}\)
    • Exactly. When \(\omega \neq \omega_0\) (as with \(k = 8\)) we get the standard case and a bounded particular solution. When \(\omega = \omega_0\) (as with \(k = 9\)) the homogeneous equation takes the form \(x'' + \omega^2 x = 0\text{,}\) so the standard guess already solves it: we are in the case of duplication, the factor of \(t\) appears in \(x_p\text{,}\) and the two solutions look completely different. On the next page we see what this means physically. \(\checkmark\)
    • The second example had a larger value of \(k\text{,}\) which made the ODE harder to solve.
    • The size of \(k\) alone is not what caused the difference. What mattered was the relationship between \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k}\) and the forcing frequency \(\omega\text{:}\) specifically, whether they were equal or not.
    • The second example required the duplication rule because the right-hand side was more complicated.
    • The right-hand side is \(50\cos(3t)\) in both examples. The duplication arose because the natural frequency \(\omega_0 = 3\) happened to match the forcing frequency \(\omega = 3\text{,}\) not because of anything different about the right-hand side.
    • In the first example \(B = 0\) dropped out, making it a simpler calculation.
    • That \(B = 0\) in the first example is a nice simplification, but it is not the fundamental difference between the two cases. The real distinction is whether the forcing frequency matches the natural frequency.
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Two cases: When the forcing frequency \(\omega\) and the natural frequency \(\omega_0\) are different, undetermined coefficients works in the standard way. When \(\omega = \omega_0\text{,}\) we hit the case of duplication and must multiply the guess by \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Frequencies are the key: The case of duplication arises precisely because the external force is driving the system at its own natural frequency (that is, \(\omega=\omega_0\)). On the next page we see what this means physically.
