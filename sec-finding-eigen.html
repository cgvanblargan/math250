    Section 5.4.2 Finding Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

    So far we have verified whether a given vector was an eigenvector of a matrix (and from that we could see the eigenvalue). But without already being given the eigenvector, we still have a way of finding the eigenvalues! Again, we will borrow some facts from linear algebra, and won’t have time to fully justify all of it.
    🔗

    Fact 5.4.1. Computing Eigenvalues.

    \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) exactly when \(\lambda\) satisfies:
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - \lambda I) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Justification on why solving \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \) finds the eigenvalues (can skim).

    Notice that \(A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\) is equivalent to:
    \begin{equation*} (A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    So \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue exactly when this equation has a non-zero solution \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A standard result from linear algebra says this happens exactly when the determinant of the matrix \(A - \lambda I\) is zero.
    🔗
    🔗
    To find \(A - \lambda I\text{,}\) we note that subtracting \(\lambda I\) just subtracts \(\lambda\) along the main diagonal of \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A - \lambda I \amp= \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix} - \lambda \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} \lambda \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \lambda \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{bmatrix} a - \lambda \amp b \\ c \amp d - \lambda \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    So the steps for finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors are:
    1. Subtract \(\lambda\) along the diagonal to compute \(A - \lambda I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Take the determinant of \(A - \lambda I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Set the determinant equal to \(0\text{,}\) and solve for \(\lambda\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Last, find the eigenvectors that go with each eigenvalue.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    First we do an example with steps 1-3 to find the eigenvalues of a matrix.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.2. Finding the Eigenvalues.

    Find the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We will break this up into two steps. Step 1 is to compute \(\det(A - \lambda I)\text{.}\) Step 2 is to set it equal to \(0\) and solve for \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1. Step 1: Computing \(\det(A - \lambda I)\)
    Subtracting \(\lambda\) along the diagonal and computing the determinant:
    \begin{align*} \det(A - \lambda I) \amp= \begin{vmatrix} 4 - \lambda \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 - \lambda \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp= (4 - \lambda)(-1 - \lambda) - 2 \cdot 3\\ \amp= -4 - 3\lambda + \lambda^2 - 6\\ \amp= \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 10. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In step one, we found that \(\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 10\text{,}\) a quadratic in \(\lambda\text{.}\) Now we solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 2. Step 2: Solving \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\)
    Setting the quadratic equal to \(0\) and factoring:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda + 2) = 0. \end{equation*}
    So the eigenvalues are \(\lambda = 5\) and \(\lambda = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that \(\lambda = -2\) is exactly the eigenvalue we confirmed in Exploration 5.4.1; this time we found it from scratch, along with a second eigenvalue \(\lambda = 5\) that we didn’t know about before. Also notice that \(\det(A - \lambda I)\) came out as a quadratic in \(\lambda\text{.}\) That will always happen for a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, so we can expect (at most) two eigenvalues from this process.
    🔗
    Now, how do we find the eigenvector that goes with each eigenvalue? We return to the second form of the definition:
    \begin{equation*} (A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}, \end{equation*}
    but this time \(\lambda\) is a known number, and the unknown is the vector \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Plugging in the eigenvalue turns this into a system of equations for \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) that we can solve. Let’s do \(\lambda = 5\) together.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.3. Finding an Eigenvector for \(\lambda = 5\).

    Find an eigenvector of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\) associated with the eigenvalue \(\lambda = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We will break this up into two steps. Step 1 will come from solving \((A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) Step 2 involves solving for a specific eigenvector.
    🔗
    Solution 1. Step 1: Solving \((A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\)
    We solve \((A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) Subtracting \(5\) along the diagonal:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 5 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 - 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -6 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Which means we need to solve the matrix-vector equation:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Converting to a system of equations (from each row of the equation above):
    \begin{align*} -v_1 + 2v_2 \amp= 0\\ 3v_1 - 6v_2 \amp= 0. \end{align*}
    Notice that the second equation is just \(-3\) times the first! So, both equations are equivalent, and so the solutions can be described just by the first equation:
    \begin{equation*} -v_1 + 2v_2 = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In step one, we discovered that eigenvectors for \(A\) satisfy the relationship
    \begin{equation*} -v_1 + 2v_2 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Now we will find a specific eigenvector.
    🔗
    Solution 2. Step 2: Finding a specific eigenvector
    We can rewrite \(-v_1 + 2v_2 = 0\) as
    \begin{equation*} 2v_2 = v_1. \end{equation*}
    Notice that there are infinitely many solutions to this. We can fix either component and solve for the other. Let’s set \(v_1 = 1\text{.}\) Then \(2v_2 = v_1 = 1\) gives \(v_2 = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) and:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = 5 \implies \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Notice, any non-zero multiple also gives a correct answer, for example: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1/2 \end{bmatrix}\) all work. (In fact, \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is arguably the nicest, since it avoids fractions.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Observation 5.4.4. Two Things to Notice From the Example.

    • The equations will be redundant. When \(\lambda\) really is an eigenvalue, the two equations in the system will always be equivalent (this follows from theory in linear algebra we won’t go into). So, double check they are in fact multiples of each other, and then choose just one equation to find an eigenvector.
      🔗
      🔗
    • You get to make a choice of fixing either \(v_1\) or \(v_2\text{.}\) In the previous example, we chose \(v_1 = 1\text{,}\) which determined a specific value for \(v_2\text{.}\) We could’ve chosen any other non-zero number for \(v_1\text{.}\) Alternatively, we could’ve fixed \(v_2\) first, instead.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now you can try finding an eigenvector associated with the other eigenvalue, \(\lambda = -2\text{!}\) The following activity walks through the same process, one step at a time.
    🔗

    Activity 5.4.1. Finding an Eigenvector for \(\lambda = -2\).

    We will find an eigenvector of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\) associated with the eigenvalue \(\lambda = -2\text{,}\) by solving \((A - (-2) I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a) Compute \(A - (-2)I\).

    What is the matrix \(A - (-2)I\text{?}\) (Careful: subtracting \(-2\) means adding \(2\text{!}\))
    🔗
    • \(A - (-2)I = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Correct! Subtracting \(-2\) along the diagonal adds \(2\) to the diagonal entries: \(4 - (-2) = 6\) and \(-1 - (-2) = 1\text{.}\) The off-diagonal entries are untouched.
    • \(A - (-2)I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Watch the double negative! This matrix comes from subtracting \(+2\) along the diagonal, but here \(\lambda = -2\text{,}\) so the diagonal entries are \(4 - (-2) = 4 + 2 = 6\) and \(-1 - (-2) = -1 + 2 = 1\text{.}\)
    • \(A - (-2)I = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \amp 4 \\ 5 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Careful: \(\lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \lambda \end{bmatrix}\) only has entries on the diagonal, so only the diagonal entries of \(A\) change. The off-diagonal entries \(2\) and \(3\) should stay the same.
    🔗

    (b) Convert to a System of Equations.

    When solving \(\begin{bmatrix} 6 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) what does the system of equations become?
    🔗
    • \begin{align*} 6v_1 + 2v_2 \amp= 0\\ 3v_1 + v_2 \amp= 0 \end{align*}
    • Correct! Each row of the matrix gives one equation: the top row gives \(6v_1 + 2v_2 = 0\text{,}\) and the bottom row gives \(3v_1 + v_2 = 0\text{.}\) As expected, the equations are redundant: the first is just \(2\) times the second.
    • \begin{align*} 6v_1 + 3v_2 \amp= 0\\ 2v_1 + v_2 \amp= 0 \end{align*}
    • Close, but this uses the columns of the matrix. Each equation comes from a row: multiply across the row and down the vector, so the top row gives \(6v_1 + 2v_2 = 0\text{.}\)
    • \begin{align*} 6v_1 + 2v_2 \amp= v_1\\ 3v_1 + v_2 \amp= v_2 \end{align*}
    • Careful with the right-hand side: we are solving \((A - (-2)I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) and the \(\lambda\mathbf{v}\) part has already been moved to the left side inside \(A - (-2)I\text{.}\) So the right-hand side of both equations is just \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (c) Find a Specific Eigenvector.

    Since the two equations are redundant, we can just use the first equation: \(6v_1 + 2v_2 = 0\text{.}\) This time (unlike the example, where we fixed \(v_1\)) let’s fix the second component. Choosing \(v_2 = 3\text{,}\) what is the resulting eigenvector?
    🔗
    • \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Correct! With \(v_2 = 3\text{,}\) the equation becomes \(6v_1 + 6 = 0\text{,}\) so \(v_1 = -1\text{.}\) Notice this is \(-1\) times the eigenvector \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\) we confirmed back on the previous page. Both are perfectly valid eigenvectors, since any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector!
    • \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Close, but watch the sign when solving. With \(v_2 = 3\text{,}\) the equation is \(6v_1 + 2 \cdot 3 = 0\text{,}\) so \(6v_1 = -6\) and \(v_1 = -1\text{,}\) not \(+1\text{.}\) You can also check directly: \(A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) which is not a multiple of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    • \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • It looks like the components got swapped! We fixed the second component \(v_2 = 3\text{,}\) and solving \(6v_1 + 6 = 0\) gives the first component \(v_1 = -1\text{.}\) Checking directly: \(A\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) which is not a multiple of \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Putting it all together, we now know everything eigen-related about this matrix:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1 = -2 \text{ with } \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_2 = 5 \text{ with } \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    (Here we chose \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\) rather than the \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) from the activity; since one is a multiple of the other, either is a fine choice.) Hold onto this matrix and these answers; they are about to make a reappearance when we return to differential equations in the next section.
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Solve for eigenvalues first: solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\text{.}\) For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix this is a quadratic in \(\lambda\text{,}\) so expect two eigenvalues.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Then find the eigenvectors: for each eigenvalue, plug it into \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) convert to a system of equations, and solve for the ratio between \(v_1\) and \(v_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The two equations when finding eigenvectors are redundant: the two equations in the system will always be multiples of each other when \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue. So, you can just choose one of the equations (coming from the rows) to solve for an eigenvector.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Fix a convenient value for either \(v_1\) or \(v_2\text{:}\) choose any non-zero value for one of the components, such as \(v_1 = 1\text{,}\) which will determine the other component. This will give you one specific eigenvector (there are infinitely many).
      🔗
      🔗
