    Section 5.3.1 The Determinant and the Wronskian

    We found two solutions to a system on the last page, and hinted that they can be combined to build the general solution. To make that precise, we need two more tools from linear algebra: the determinant and linear independence.
    🔗
    Keep in mind that in a linear algebra course, these ideas get weeks of development and carry a lot of theory. We only need a working understanding for ODEs, so we won’t go deep into the theory. If you haven’t taken a linear algebra or matrices course, then you may find this section a little dense.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.1. Determinant.

    If \(A\) is a \(2 \times 2\) matrix in the form \(\begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then the determinant of \(A\) is:
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \begin{vmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The straight bars mean we’re taking the determinant of the matrix. There are a lot of important interpretations and consequences of the determinant. What we need is the connection to linear independence.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.2. Linear Independence.

    Two vectors \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are linearly independent if the only solution to:
    \begin{equation*} c_1\mathbf{x} + c_2\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0} \end{equation*}
    is when \(c_1 = c_2 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In general there is a lot to unpack with linear independence, but with just two vectors, \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are linearly independent exactly when they are not multiples of each other. And when that happens, the linear combinations \(c_1\mathbf{x} + c_2\mathbf{y}\) realize every vector in the plane as you vary over all \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{.}\) That is, every vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can be built out of two linearly independent vectors.
    🔗
    The determinant gives us a quick test for linear independence:
    🔗

    Fact 5.3.3. Determinant Test for Independence.

    The columns of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix are linearly independent if and only if \(\det(A) \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We will apply this idea in the following example.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.4. Computing a Determinant.

    Compute the determinant of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Are the columns linearly independent?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the formula:
    \begin{equation*} \det = \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 4 \end{vmatrix} = 1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3 = 4 - 6 = -2. \end{equation*}
    Since \(-2 \neq 0\text{,}\) the columns \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\) are linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Using the same process as the example, complete the following checkpoint.
    🔗

    Activity 5.3.1. Determinant Checkpoint.

    Compute the determinant of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 4 \amp 8 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Are the columns linearly independent?
    🔗
    • \(\det = 0\text{,}\) so the columns are not linearly independent.
    • Correct! \(\det = 1 \cdot 8 - 2 \cdot 4 = 8 - 8 = 0\text{,}\) so the columns are not linearly independent. In fact, you can see it directly: the second column \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}\) is exactly \(2\) times the first column \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) They’re multiples of each other!
    • \(\det = 0\text{,}\) so the columns are linearly independent.
    • The determinant is right, but the conclusion is backwards! The test says the columns are linearly independent when \(\det \neq 0\text{.}\) Here \(\det = 0\text{,}\) so the columns are not independent. Indeed, the second column is \(2\) times the first.
    • \(\det = 16\text{,}\) so the columns are linearly independent.
    • Check the formula: it’s \(ad - bc\text{,}\) with a minus sign. Here that’s \(1 \cdot 8 - 2 \cdot 4 = 0\text{,}\) not \(8 + 8 = 16\text{.}\) Since the determinant is \(0\text{,}\) the columns are not linearly independent.
    🔗
    Let’s return to solutions of systems. As before, we are only going to scratch the surface of the underlying theory. Instead, most of our effort will go towards applying these ideas in future sections!
    🔗

    Fact 5.3.5. The Principle of Superposition.

    With homogeneous linear systems, we still have a principle of superposition. That is, if \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are solutions to:
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= p_{11}(t)x_1 + p_{12}(t)x_2\\ x_2' \amp= p_{21}(t)x_1 + p_{22}(t)x_2 \end{align*}
    (written in vector form as \(\mathbf{x}' = P(t)\mathbf{x}\)), then any linear combination:
    \begin{equation*} c_1\mathbf{x} + c_2\mathbf{y} \end{equation*}
    is also a solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    (You can verify this by plugging \(c_1\mathbf{x} + c_2\mathbf{y}\) into the system, just like we did for second-order equations.)
    🔗
    Let’s connect this back to Example 5.2.1, where we verified that
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{y}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{z}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    are both solutions to the homogeneous system \(\mathbf{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -3 \\ 6 \amp -7 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\text{.}\) The principle of superposition lets us build many more solutions out of these two.
    🔗

    Activity 5.3.2. Superposition Checkpoint.

    Which of the following is an infinite family of solutions built from \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Correct! This is \(c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z}\text{.}\) By the principle of superposition, every choice of the constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) produces a solution, so this is infinitely many solutions all at once.
    • \(c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Look closely: these two vectors are not \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\text{!}\) Their components have been mixed together, and these mixed vectors are not solutions to the system, so superposition doesn’t apply. Each solution keeps its own exponential in both components: \(\mathbf{y}\) is all \(e^{2t}\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) is all \(e^{-5t}\text{.}\)
    • \(c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 2e^{2t} \\ 3e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{-5t} \\ e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Careful: the components of each vector have been flipped upside down! The solution \(\mathbf{y}\) has \(3e^{2t}\) on top and \(2e^{2t}\) on the bottom (and similarly for \(\mathbf{z}\)). A flipped vector is generally not a solution anymore, so superposition only applies to the original \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So from just two solutions, we instantly get infinitely many. This raises the question: when does \(c_1\mathbf{x} + c_2\mathbf{y}\) give us all of the solutions?
    🔗
    It turns out this is when \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are linearly independent. This is closely related to what we said above about vectors: two linearly independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are enough to build every vector in the plane through their linear combinations. The situation with solutions is analogous: two "independent enough" solutions are enough to build every solution. We won’t go further into the theory here; the takeaway is that we need our two solutions to be linearly independent, and we need a way to check for that.
    🔗
    We will check linear independence using the Wronskian. It’s exactly the determinant test from above, applied to our two solutions.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.6. The Wronskian.

    Suppose \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) The Wronskian, \(W = W(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\text{,}\) is:
    \begin{equation*} W = \det\left( \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \amp y_1 \\ x_2 \amp y_2 \end{bmatrix} \right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice the two solutions become the columns of the matrix. And finally, we get to our main theorem of the section by combining the previous results!
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.7. The Wronskian and the General Solution.

    If \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are two solutions to a first-order, linear, homogeneous system such that:
    \begin{equation*} W(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \neq 0, \end{equation*}
    then the general solution is given by \(c_1\mathbf{x} + c_2\mathbf{y}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s apply the theorem to our two solutions from above. Recall they were
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{y}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{z}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Placing \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) as the columns and computing:
    \begin{align*} W(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{z}) \amp= \det\left( \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \amp e^{-5t} \\ 2e^{2t} \amp 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} \right)\\ \amp= 3e^{2t} \cdot 3e^{-5t} - e^{-5t} \cdot 2e^{2t}\\ \amp= 9e^{-3t} - 2e^{-3t} = 7e^{-3t}. \end{align*}
    Notice that \(W\) involves \(t\) here (that will often happen). The question is whether \(W\) can ever be \(0\text{.}\) Since \(e^{-3t}\) is never \(0\text{,}\) we have \(W = 7e^{-3t} \neq 0\) for any \(t\text{.}\) So the theorem applies:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}=c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 3e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t} \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} e^{-5t} \\ 3e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    is the general solution (it captures all of the solutions to the system).
    🔗
    On the next page, we’ll run this whole process from start to finish on a new system: verify two solutions, check the Wronskian, and write down the general solution.
