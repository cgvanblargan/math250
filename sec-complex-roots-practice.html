    Section 3.1.5 Complex Roots and Practice

    Recall that for \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\text{,}\) if the characteristic equation has complex roots \(\alpha \pm \beta i\text{,}\) the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = e^{\alpha x}\left(c_1\cos(\beta x) + c_2\sin(\beta x)\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.1. Why Does the Complex Root Case Give Sines and Cosines?

    If the characteristic equation has complex roots \(\alpha \pm \beta i\text{,}\) then \(y_1 = e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)\) and \(y_2 = e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)\) are two linearly independent real-valued solutions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Justification (can skim for now).

    From the complex roots \(r = \alpha \pm \beta i\text{,}\) we formally get complex solutions \(e^{(\alpha + \beta i)x}\) and \(e^{(\alpha - \beta i)x}\text{.}\) Using Euler’s formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} e^{(\alpha + \beta i)x} \amp= e^{\alpha x}e^{i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x))\\ e^{(\alpha - \beta i)x} \amp= e^{\alpha x}e^{-i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x)) \end{align*}
    Taking the sum and difference of these two complex solutions (and dividing by appropriate constants) gives two real-valued solutions:
    \begin{align*} y_1 \amp= e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)\\ y_2 \amp= e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x) \end{align*}
    These are linearly independent since one involves cosine and the other sine, and neither is a constant multiple of the other (assuming \(\beta \neq 0\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    A key difference in the solution for this case is that it involves \(\sin(x)\) and \(\cos(x)\text{.}\) Let’s explore a simple case to see how these trig functions naturally appear. It is tempting to jump straight to the characteristic equation, but solving this one by inspection actually gives you stronger intuition for why the solution involves trig functions. Even more, ODEs similar to the following example appear often in applications we will explore later, and it is useful to be able to jump straight to the solution without using the characteristic equation.
    🔗

    Exploration 3.1.1. Exploring \(y'' = -y\).

    Consider the ODE \(y'' = -y\text{,}\) or equivalently \(y'' + y = 0\text{.}\) This is asking: what functions have a second derivative equal to the negative of themselves? Let’s find the general solution two different ways.
    🔗

    (a) Part 1: By Inspection.

    We’re looking for functions whose second derivative equals the negative of the function itself. Can you think of two functions that satisfy this?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Think about what you know from calculus. Which familiar functions have the property that their second derivative is the negative of themselves?
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We know from calculus that:
    \begin{align*} \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cos(x) \amp= -\cos(x)\\ \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\sin(x) \amp= -\sin(x) \end{align*}
    So both \(y_1 = \cos(x)\) and \(y_2 = \sin(x)\) satisfy \(y'' = -y\text{.}\) Since they are not constant multiples of each other, they are linearly independent, and the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1\cos(x) + c_2\sin(x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Part 2: Via the Characteristic Equation.

    Now let’s arrive at the same answer using the characteristic equation for \(y'' + y = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 + 1 = 0\text{.}\) This doesn’t factor easily, but you don’t need the quadratic formula either. Just solve directly for \(r^2\text{!}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 + 1 = 0\text{.}\) Solving directly:
    \begin{align*} r^2 + 1 \amp= 0\\ r^2 \amp= -1\\ r \amp= \pm i \end{align*}
    So the roots are \(r = 0 \pm 1 \cdot i\text{,}\) giving \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 1\text{.}\) The general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = e^{0 \cdot x}\left(c_1\cos(1 \cdot x) + c_2\sin(1 \cdot x)\right) = c_1\cos(x) + c_2\sin(x). \end{equation*}
    Just as we found by inspection!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solving the ODE by inspection came naturally from understanding how the derivatives of sine and cosine work. Furthermore, when we solved the characteristic equation, the real part of the root was 0, which is precisely why there was no exponential in the final answer. In general, when the roots are \(\alpha \pm \beta i\text{,}\) the real part \(\alpha\) shows up in the exponential and the imaginary part \(\beta\) appears inside the cosine and sine.
    🔗
    If you worked through the example above but replaced \(y'' + y = 0\) with any equation of the form \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\text{,}\) you would arrive at a very similar result, stated in the observation below.
    🔗

    Observation 3.1.2. The ODE \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\).

    For any constant \(\omega \neq 0\text{,}\) the ODE
    \begin{equation*} y'' + \omega^2 y = 0 \end{equation*}
    has characteristic equation \(r^2 + \omega^2 = 0\text{,}\) giving roots \(r = \pm \omega i\) (so \(\alpha = 0\text{,}\) \(\beta = \omega\)). The general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1\cos(\omega x) + c_2\sin(\omega x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    With this observation, you should be able to solve the following checkpoint question with almost no work!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.3.

    Using the observation above, what is the general solution to \(y'' + 16y = 0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(y = c_1\cos(4x) + c_2\sin(4x)\)
    • Exactly! This fits the form \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) with \(\omega = 4\text{,}\) so the general solution is \(y = c_1\cos(4x) + c_2\sin(4x)\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    • \(y = c_1 e^{4x} + c_2 e^{-4x}\)
    • This would be the solution to \(y'' - 16y = 0\text{,}\) not \(y'' + 16y = 0\text{.}\) The positive sign on the \(16y\) term gives complex roots, not real ones. Always check the sign before deciding which case you’re in!
    🔗
    Now for some more practice! Pay close attention to the difference between parts (c) and (d).
    🔗

    Activity 3.1.2. Practice: All Cases.

    (a) Solve \(y'' - 4y' + 5y = 0\).

    Find the general solution to \(y'' - 4y' + 5y = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 - 4r + 5 = 0\text{.}\) This does not factor easily, so use the quadratic formula.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 - 4r + 5 = 0\text{.}\) Applying the quadratic formula:
    \begin{align*} r \amp= \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 20}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 2i}{2} = 2 \pm i. \end{align*}
    So \(\alpha = 2\) and \(\beta = 1\text{.}\) The general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = e^{2x}\left(c_1\cos(x) + c_2\sin(x)\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Solve \(y'' - 5y' + 4y = 0\).

    Find the general solution to \(y'' - 5y' + 4y = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 - 5r + 4 = 0\text{.}\) Try factoring first!
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 - 5r + 4 = 0\text{,}\) which factors as:
    \begin{equation*} (r - 1)(r - 4) = 0. \end{equation*}
    So \(r_1 = 1\) and \(r_2 = 4\text{.}\) The general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1 e^{x} + c_2 e^{4x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) Solve \(y'' + 9y = 0\).

    Find the general solution to \(y'' + 9y = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Notice this fits the form \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) from Observation 3.1.2. Just identify \(\omega\) and write down the solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    This fits the form \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) with \(\omega = 3\text{.}\) By Observation 3.1.2, the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1\cos(3x) + c_2\sin(3x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) Solve \(y'' - 9y = 0\).

    Find the general solution to \(y'' - 9y = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Notice this does not follow from Observation 3.1.2, since the coefficient of \(y\) is \(-9\text{,}\) not \(+9\text{.}\) Find the roots of the characteristic equation: you may be surprised which case you’re in!
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is \(r^2 - 9 = 0\text{,}\) which factors as:
    \begin{equation*} (r - 3)(r + 3) = 0. \end{equation*}
    So \(r_1 = 3\) and \(r_2 = -3\text{,}\) which are two distinct real roots, not complex! The general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1 e^{3x} + c_2 e^{-3x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.4.

    In part (d) above, \(y'' - 9y = 0\) looks similar to \(y'' + 9y = 0\) from part (c), yet the solutions look completely different. Why can’t we apply Observation 3.1.2 to \(y'' - 9y = 0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • The observation requires the coefficient of \(y\) to be positive. Since \(y'' - 9y = 0\) has a negative coefficient, the characteristic equation \(r^2 - 9 = 0\) has real roots, not complex ones, so trig functions do not appear.
    • Exactly. Observation 3.1.2 applies only when the ODE has the form \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) with a positive coefficient. A negative coefficient means the characteristic equation has real roots, giving exponentials instead of trig functions. \(\checkmark\)
    • The observation only applies when the coefficient of \(y\) is exactly 9.
    • Not quite. The observation applies for any positive \(\omega^2\text{,}\) not just 9. The issue with \(y'' - 9y = 0\) is the sign, not the specific value.
    • The observation does apply, but the answer happens to come out as exponentials anyway.
    • The observation does not apply here. It requires a positive coefficient on \(y\text{,}\) which gives complex roots and trig functions. With a negative coefficient the characteristic equation has real roots from the start, so exponentials are the correct form.
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Complex roots \(\alpha \pm \beta i\text{:}\) the general solution is \(y = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\beta x) + c_2\sin(\beta x))\text{.}\) The real part \(\alpha\) goes in the exponential, the imaginary part \(\beta\) goes inside the trig.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Know this one cold: the ODE \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) has general solution \(y = c_1\cos(\omega x) + c_2\sin(\omega x)\text{.}\) You should be able to write this down immediately without touching the characteristic equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Sign matters: \(y'' + 9y = 0\) gives trig functions, but \(y'' - 9y = 0\) gives exponentials. Always check the sign of the coefficient before assuming which case you’re in.
