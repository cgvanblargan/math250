    Section 3.3.5 Damping cases and the constants c, m, k

    We’ve now seen all three damping cases, underdamped, overdamped, and critically damped, plus the undamped case from before. Now that we have done the hard work, we can summarize all of the behavior we discovered!
    🔗

    Summary of Behavior: \(mx'' + cx' + kx = 0\).

    We solve this ODE by solving the roots of the characteristic equation:
    \begin{equation*} mr^2 + cr + k = 0. \end{equation*}
    The behavior of solutions is determined entirely by the discriminant \(D = c^2 - 4km\text{,}\) which controls the type of roots of the characteristic equation.
    🔗
    Case Discriminant and roots Crossings of \(x=0\) \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty} x(t)\)
    Overdamped (larger \(c\)) \(c^2 > 4km\) (two distinct real roots) At most once \(0\)
    Critically damped (borderline) \(c^2 = 4km\) (one repeated real root) At most once \(0\)
    Underdamped (smaller \(c\)) \(0 \lt c^2 \lt 4km\) (complex roots with nonzero real part) Infinitely many \(0\)
    Undamped (\(c = 0\)) \(c = 0\) (pure imaginary roots) Infinitely many DNE
    In all damped cases (\(c > 0\)), the mass eventually approaches equilibrium: \(\lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = 0\text{.}\) In the undamped case (\(c = 0\)) the mass oscillates forever with constant amplitude.
    🔗
    🔗
    As seen in the second column, you can determine what case we are in simply by knowing \(m\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) and \(k\text{.}\) See if you can apply this to the next question!
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.1.

    A spring-mass-dashpot system has \(m = 1\text{,}\) \(c = 3\text{,}\) \(k = 4\text{.}\) Which damping case is this?
    🔗
    • Underdamped
    • Correct! The discriminant is \(c^2 - 4km = 9 - 16 = -7 \lt 0\text{,}\) so the characteristic equation has complex roots and the system is underdamped. \(\checkmark\)
    • Overdamped
    • Overdamped requires \(c^2 > 4km\text{.}\) Here \(c^2 - 4km = 9 - 16 = -7 \lt 0\text{,}\) so this is not overdamped.
    • Critically damped
    • Critically damped requires \(c^2 = 4km\text{.}\) Here \(c^2 - 4km = -7 \neq 0\text{.}\)
    • Undamped
    • Undamped means \(c = 0\text{.}\) Here \(c = 3 \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let’s go the other direction: starting from the behavior of the system, can we narrow down which cases are possible?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.2.

    The mass passes through the equilibrium position infinitely many times. Select all cases this could correspond to.
    🔗
    • Underdamped
    • Correct! Underdamped solutions oscillate with decaying amplitude and cross \(x = 0\) infinitely many times. \(\checkmark\)
    • Undamped
    • Correct! The undamped system oscillates with constant amplitude and also crosses \(x = 0\) infinitely many times. \(\checkmark\)
    • Overdamped
    • Overdamped solutions pass through equilibrium at most once. They cannot cross infinitely many times.
    • Critically damped
    • Critically damped solutions also pass through equilibrium at most once.
    🔗
    Let’s combine these ideas into a more involved example, where we have to work backward from the desired behavior to figure out which values of \(c\) make it happen.
    🔗

    Example 3.3.3. Identifying the Damping Case.

    Suppose that the position of a mass in a spring-mass-dashpot system is modeled by the ODE:
    \begin{equation*} x'' + cx' + 4x = 0, \quad x(0) = 1,\quad x'(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    For what values of \(c\) will the mass oscillate and pass through the equilibrium position infinitely many times?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Which damping cases cause the mass to pass through equilibrium infinitely many times? What conditions on \(c\) correspond to those cases?
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the summary above, the mass passes through equilibrium infinitely many times when the system is underdamped or undamped. Here \(m = 1\) and \(k = 4\text{,}\) so the discriminant is \(D = c^2 - 4km = c^2 - 16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Underdamped requires \(0 \lt c^2 \lt 4km\text{,}\) i.e. \(0 \lt c^2 \lt 16\text{,}\) which gives \(0 \lt c \lt 4\text{.}\) Undamped requires \(c = 0\text{.}\) Combining, the mass oscillates infinitely many times when
    \begin{equation*} 0 \leq c \lt 4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose \(c = 4\text{.}\) Can you make the mass oscillate and pass through the equilibrium position infinitely many times by choosing a different value for \(x'(0)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    The damping case depends only on an inequality involving \(c\text{,}\) \(m\text{,}\) and \(k\text{.}\) If these are fixed, does changing the initial conditions affect which case we are in?
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    No. The damping case is determined entirely by \(c^2 - 4km\text{,}\) which depends only on \(m\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) and \(k\text{.}\) With \(c = 4\text{,}\) \(m = 1\text{,}\) \(k = 4\) we have \(D = 16 - 16 = 0\text{,}\) so the system is critically damped regardless of the initial conditions. A critically damped system passes through equilibrium at most once, so no choice of \(x'(0)\) can produce infinitely many crossings.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The second part of that example hints at something worth pinning down more carefully: the type of roots alone doesn’t always tell us a unique case. Let’s see this in action with complex roots.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.4.

    The characteristic equation of a spring-mass system has complex roots. Which case or cases could this correspond to?
    🔗
    • Undamped or underdamped
    • Correct! Complex roots arise in two situations. When \(c = 0\) the roots are purely imaginary (zero real part) and the system is undamped. When \(c > 0\) and \(c^2 \lt 4km\) the roots have a nonzero negative real part and the system is underdamped. In both cases the roots are complex. \(\checkmark\)
    • Underdamped only
    • Underdamped is one correct case, but not the only one. When \(c = 0\) the characteristic equation also has complex (purely imaginary) roots, giving the undamped case.
    • Overdamped
    • Overdamped corresponds to two distinct real roots, not complex roots.
    • Critically damped
    • Critically damped corresponds to one repeated real root, not complex roots.
    🔗
    Okay, so we now have a solid understanding of the different types of solutions. In the next section, we take this a step further and analyze the behavior of solutions in more detail!
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • The damping case tells us: how many times the mass crosses the equilibrium position and the long-term behavior \(\lim_{t\to\infty} x(t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The damping case is determined by the type of roots of the characteristic equation, which can be found by computing the discriminant \(D = c^2 - 4km\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Initial conditions do not change the case: \(m\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) and \(k\) alone determine whether the system is overdamped, critically damped, or underdamped.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Complex roots arise in two cases: purely imaginary roots (\(c = 0\text{,}\) undamped) and complex roots with negative real part (\(c > 0\) and \(c^2 \lt 4km\text{,}\) underdamped).
