    Section 3.1.2 The Characteristic Equation

    Let’s continue looking at the ODE \(y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0\) from the previous page. Notice that this is a 2nd-order linear homogeneous ODE. However, it is a pretty simple one as the coefficient functions are all constant. More precisely, \(P_0(x)=1\text{,}\) \(P_1(x)=-5\text{,}\) and \(P_2(x)=6\text{.}\) These types of ODEs will be the focus for the rest of this chapter, and are perhaps the most fun ones to solve yet!
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.1. Second-Order Linear Homogeneous ODE with Constant Coefficients.

    A second-order linear homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients has the form
    \begin{equation*} ay'' + by' + cy = 0, \end{equation*}
    where \(a \neq 0\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) are constants.
    🔗
    🔗
    On the previous page we saw that \(y_1 = e^{2x}\) and \(y_2 = e^{3x}\) are solutions to \(y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0\text{,}\) and by superposition, \(y = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{3x}\) is also a solution for any constants \(c_1, c_2\text{.}\) How do we know if this is all of the solutions?
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.2. General Solution of a Second-Order Linear Homogeneous ODE.

    Suppose \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are two linearly independent solutions to a second-order linear homogeneous ODE (meaning \(y_1 \neq k y_2\) for any constant \(k\)). Then
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2 \end{equation*}
    is the general solution to the ODE (meaning this family of solutions contains all possible solutions).
    🔗
    🔗
    We won’t prove this theorem in this course, but we will be making heavy use of it. For example, now we can say for certain that \(y = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{3x}\) gives every solution to \(y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0\) as we vary over all real numbers for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.3.

    It turns out that \(y_1 = e^{x}\) and \(y_2 = e^{2x}\) are two solutions to \(y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0\text{.}\) What is the general solution?
    🔗
    • \(y = c_1 e^{x} + c_2 e^{2x}\)
    • Exactly! Since \(y_1 = e^x\) and \(y_2 = e^{2x}\) are solutions that are also linearly independent (not constant multiples of each other), the general solution is \(y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2 = c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{2x}\text{.}\) Every solution to this ODE can be written this way. \(\checkmark\)
    • \(y = e^{x} + e^{2x}\)
    • Close, but this is just one specific solution (with \(c_1 = c_2 = 1\)). The general solution needs arbitrary constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) to capture all possible solutions.
    • \(y = c_1 e^{x} \cdot c_2 e^{2x}\)
    • Not quite. The general solution uses a sum of the two solutions, not a product. Superposition tells us \(c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2\) is a solution, not \(c_1 y_1 \cdot c_2 y_2\text{.}\)
    • \(y = c_1 e^{3x}\)
    • This only has one arbitrary constant, so it can’t be the general solution to a second-order ODE. We always need two linearly independent solutions and two constants.
    🔗
    So the theorem tells us that finding two linearly independent solutions is the whole game. But how do we actually find solutions in the first place? We will explore this below, and soon we will have a way to systematically solve these ODEs.
    🔗

    Exploration 3.1.1. Where Do Solutions Come From? Trying \(y = e^{rx}\).

    Consider the ODE \(y'' - 3y' - 4y = 0\text{.}\) From the previous page, we might guess that some solutions take the form \(y = e^{rx}\text{.}\) Let’s suppose \(y = e^{rx}\) for some unknown constant \(r\text{,}\) and figure out which values of \(r\) make it a solution (this should look familiar from chapter 1!).
    🔗

    (a) Step 1: Find \(y'\) and \(y''\).

    If \(y = e^{rx}\text{,}\) what are \(y'\) and \(y''\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} y \amp= e^{rx}\\ y' \amp= re^{rx}\\ y'' \amp= r^2 e^{rx} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Step 2: Substitute into the ODE.

    Substitute \(y = e^{rx}\text{,}\) \(y' = re^{rx}\text{,}\) \(y'' = r^2 e^{rx}\) into \(y'' - 3y' - 4y = 0\) and factor out \(e^{rx}\text{.}\) Which equation do you get?
    🔗
    • \(e^{rx}(r^2 - 3r - 4) = 0\)
    • Exactly right! Substituting gives:
      \begin{align*} r^2 e^{rx} - 3re^{rx} - 4e^{rx} \amp= 0\\ e^{rx}(r^2 - 3r - 4) \amp= 0. \checkmark \end{align*}
    • \(e^{rx}(r^2 - 3r - 4) = e^{rx}\)
    • Not quite. The right-hand side of the ODE is \(0\text{,}\) so after substituting we should have \(0\) on the right, not \(e^{rx}\text{.}\)
    • \(e^{rx}(r - 3r - 4) = 0\)
    • Almost, but check the \(y''\) term. Since \(y'' = r^2 e^{rx}\text{,}\) the first term should contribute \(r^2\text{,}\) not \(r\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (c) Step 3: The Key Observation.

    We have \(e^{rx}(r^2 - 3r - 4) = 0\text{.}\) Since \(e^{rx} \neq 0\) for any value of \(x\text{,}\) what must be true?
    🔗
    • \(r^2 - 3r - 4 = 0\)
    • Yes! Since \(e^{rx}\) is never zero, the only way the product can equal zero is if the other factor equals zero. So \(r\) must satisfy \(r^2 - 3r - 4 = 0\text{.}\) This is called the characteristic equation. \(\checkmark\)
    • \(e^{rx} = 0\)
    • Actually, \(e^{rx} \neq 0\) for any real \(x\text{:}\) exponentials are always positive! So this factor can never be zero.
    • There are no solutions of the form \(e^{rx}\text{.}\)
    • Not quite: we just need \(r^2 - 3r - 4 = 0\) to hold. If we can find values of \(r\) satisfying this, then \(e^{rx}\) is indeed a solution.
    🔗

    (d) Step 4: Solve for \(r\).

    Factor and solve \(r^2 - 3r - 4 = 0\text{.}\) What are the two values of \(r\text{,}\) and what are the corresponding solutions \(y_1\) and \(y_2\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Factoring: \((r - 4)(r + 1) = 0\text{,}\) so \(r = 4\) or \(r = -1\text{.}\) The two solutions are:
    \begin{equation*} y_1 = e^{4x} \qquad \text{and} \qquad y_2 = e^{-x}. \end{equation*}
    Since these are not multiples of each other, they are linearly independent, and the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1 e^{4x} + c_2 e^{-x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s summarize the main findings of this exploration. We were looking to find solutions in the form \(y=e^{rx}\) to
    \begin{equation*} y'' - 3y' - 4y = 0. \end{equation*}
    We solved for the \(r\) that worked by solving the algebraic equation:
    \begin{equation*} r^2 -3r - 4 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Do you see the similarities between the ODE and the quadratic equation above? This turns out to be fundamental to solving 2nd-order linear homogeneous ODEs with constant coefficients. We call it the characteristic equation.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.4. Characteristic Equation.

    The characteristic equation of the second-order linear homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients
    \begin{equation*} ay'' + by' + cy = 0 \end{equation*}
    is the quadratic equation
    \begin{equation*} ar^2 + br + c = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    If you look through the exploration we just did, it should be clear that this process will lead to the same conclusion for any ODE in the form \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\text{.}\) That is, any time \(r\) is a root of the characteristic equation \(ar^2 + br + c = 0\text{,}\) then \(e^{rx}\) is a solution to the ODE. Even more, if there are two distinct real roots, that gives us two solutions from which we can form the general solution!
    🔗

    Observation 3.1.5. Two Distinct Real Roots.

    If \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are two distinct real roots of the characteristic equation \(ar^2 + br + c = 0\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} y_1 = e^{r_1 x} \qquad \text{and} \qquad y_2 = e^{r_2 x} \end{equation*}
    are two linearly independent solutions to \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\text{,}\) and the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that this observation only applies when there are two distinct real roots to the characteristic equation. We will be looking at other cases on the next page! Let’s quickly apply this to an example.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.6. Solving \(y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0\).

    Let’s revisit the ODE from the previous page. This time, instead of being told that \(e^{2x}\) and \(e^{3x}\) are solutions, let’s find them ourselves using the characteristic equation. Find the general solution to \(y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Write down the characteristic equation by replacing \(y''\) with \(r^2\text{,}\) \(y'\) with \(r\text{,}\) and \(y\) with \(1\text{.}\) Then factor and solve for \(r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is:
    \begin{equation*} r^2 - 5r + 6 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Factoring: \((r - 2)(r - 3) = 0\text{,}\) so \(r_1 = 2\) and \(r_2 = 3\text{.}\) These are two distinct real roots, so the general solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{3x}. \end{equation*}
    Just as we found on the previous page!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • General solution: If \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are two linearly independent solutions to a second-order linear homogeneous ODE, then \(y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2\) is the general solution, meaning every solution can be written this way.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Characteristic equation: For \(ay'' + by' + cy = 0\) the characteristic equation is \(ar^2 + br + c = 0\text{.}\) Each root \(r\) gives a solution \(e^{rx}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Distinct real roots: If the characteristic equation has two distinct real roots \(r_1\) and \(r_2\text{,}\) then \(y_1 = e^{r_1 x}\) and \(y_2 = e^{r_2 x}\) are linearly independent, and the general solution is \(y = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
