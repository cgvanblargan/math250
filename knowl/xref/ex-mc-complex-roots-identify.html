Checkpoint 3.1.4.

After applying the quadratic formula to a characteristic equation, you find:
\begin{equation*} r = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-12}}{2} = 2 \pm i\sqrt{3}. \end{equation*}
What is the general solution?
🔗
  • \(y = e^{2x}\left(c_1\cos(\sqrt{3}\,x) + c_2\sin(\sqrt{3}\,x)\right)\)
  • Exactly! From \(r = 2 \pm i\sqrt{3}\) we read off \(\alpha = 2\) and \(\beta = \sqrt{3}\text{.}\) The general solution is \(y = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\beta x) + c_2\sin(\beta x)) = e^{2x}(c_1\cos(\sqrt{3}\,x) + c_2\sin(\sqrt{3}\,x))\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
  • \(y = e^{\sqrt{3}\,x}\left(c_1\cos(2x) + c_2\sin(2x)\right)\)
  • Close, but \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are swapped. From \(r = 2 \pm i\sqrt{3}\text{,}\) the real part is \(\alpha = 2\) (goes in the exponent) and the imaginary part is \(\beta = \sqrt{3}\) (goes inside the trig functions).
  • \(y = c_1\cos(\sqrt{3}\,x) + c_2\sin(\sqrt{3}\,x)\)
  • Don’t forget the \(e^{\alpha x}\) factor out front! Since \(\alpha = 2 \neq 0\text{,}\) we need \(e^{2x}\) multiplying the trig terms.
  • \(y = c_1 e^{(2 + i\sqrt{3})x} + c_2 e^{(2 - i\sqrt{3})x}\)
  • This would be the solution if we used the complex roots directly, but we want a real-valued solution. The theorem tells us to use \(e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)\) and \(e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)\) instead.
