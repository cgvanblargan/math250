    Section 5.5.4 The Case of Complex Conjugate Eigenvalues

    On the last page, we found that our system
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= 4x_1 - 3x_2\\ x_2' \amp= 3x_1 + 4x_2 \end{align*}
    has a solution \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}e^{(4 + 3i)t}\text{,}\) but it is complex-valued, and we need two real solutions \(\mathbf{y}(t)\) and \(\mathbf{z}(t)\text{.}\) Extracting them requires some complex algebra, so let’s warm up with the two things we will need.
    🔗

    Activity 5.5.1. Splitting the Exponential.

    Our solution involves \(e^{(4 + 3i)t} = e^{4t + (3t)i}\text{.}\) Based on the exponent rules that hold for real numbers, which of the following would you expect to be true?
    🔗
    • \(e^{4t + (3t)i} = e^{4t}e^{(3t)i}\)
    • Correct! The exponent rule \(e^{a + b} = e^{a}e^{b}\) carries over to complex numbers, with \(a = 4t\) and \(b = (3t)i\text{.}\) A sum in the exponent splits into a product of exponentials.
    • \(e^{4t + (3t)i} = e^{4t} + e^{(3t)i}\)
    • Careful: the exponent rule is \(e^{a + b} = e^{a}e^{b}\text{.}\) A sum in the exponent becomes a product of exponentials, not a sum. For instance, with real numbers, \(e^{1 + 1} = e^2 = e \cdot e\text{,}\) which is not \(e + e\text{.}\)
    • \(e^{4t + (3t)i} = e^{4t}e^{3t}e^{i}\)
    • Careful: \((3t)i\) is a single product, not a sum, so it cannot be split into separate exponentials. The exponent rule \(e^{a + b} = e^{a}e^{b}\) only splits at the \(+\) sign, giving \(e^{4t}e^{(3t)i}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The checkpoint leaves us with the factor \(e^{(3t)i}\text{,}\) an exponential with an imaginary exponent. Euler’s formula, which we actually used back in Chapter 3 for complex roots of characteristic equations, gives us a useful way of re-writing this expression.
    🔗

    Fact 5.5.1. Euler’s Formula.

    For any \(\theta\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i\sin(\theta). \end{equation*}
    In particular, for a complex eigenvalue \(\lambda = p + qi\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} e^{(p + qi)t} = e^{pt}e^{(qt)i} = e^{pt}\left(\cos(qt) + i\sin(qt)\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Applying Euler’s formula with \(\theta = 3t\) to the expression we just simplified in the checkpoint:
    \begin{equation*} e^{(4 + 3i)t} = e^{4t}e^{(3t)i} = e^{4t}\left(\cos(3t) + i\sin(3t)\right). \end{equation*}
    Expressions like \(\cos(3t) + i\sin(3t)\) will need to be multiplied into the components of the eigenvector. One more warm-up before the main example!
    🔗

    Activity 5.5.2. Multiplying Out with \(i\).

    Distribute and simplify \((-i)\left(\cos(3t) + i\sin(3t)\right)\text{.}\) Which of the following is the correct result?
    🔗
    • \(\sin(3t) - i\cos(3t)\)
    • Correct! Distributing, \((-i)\cos(3t) + (-i)(i)\sin(3t) = -i\cos(3t) - i^2\sin(3t)\text{,}\) and since \(i^2 = -1\text{,}\) the last term is \(+\sin(3t)\text{.}\) Collecting the real part first: \(\sin(3t) - i\cos(3t)\text{.}\)
    • \(-\sin(3t) - i\cos(3t)\)
    • Watch the \(i^2\text{!}\) Distributing gives \(-i\cos(3t) - i^2\sin(3t)\text{,}\) and \(i^2 = -1\text{,}\) so the last term is \(-(-1)\sin(3t) = +\sin(3t)\text{,}\) not \(-\sin(3t)\text{.}\) The two negatives cancel.
    • \(-i\cos(3t) + i\sin(3t)\)
    • Careful when distributing: the second term is \((-i)(i\sin(3t)) = -i^2\sin(3t)\text{.}\) The two factors of \(i\) multiply together, and \(-i^2 = 1\text{,}\) so this term is \(+\sin(3t)\text{,}\) with no \(i\) left in it at all.
    🔗
    Now that we have established the relevant complex algebra tricks, let’s take a look at the full strategy for solving systems of ODEs with complex eigenvalues.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.5.2. The Eigenvalue Method for Complex Conjugate Eigenvalues.

    To solve \(\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}\) when \(A\) has complex conjugate eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) with corresponding eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\text{:}\)
    1. Write down the complex-valued solution \(\mathbf{v}_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Apply Euler’s formula and separate into real and imaginary parts:
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} = \mathbf{y} + i\mathbf{z}, \end{equation*}
      where \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) contain no \(i\) at all. Then it must be that \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) are themselves real-valued solutions, and they are linearly independent.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The general solution (for all real-valued solutions) is
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Alright, we can finally solve the system in the example below!
    🔗

    Example 5.5.3. Solving a System with Complex Conjugate Eigenvalues.

    Find the general solution of the system
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= 4x_1 - 3x_2\\ x_2' \amp= 3x_1 + 4x_2. \end{align*}
    You may use the eigenvalue and eigenvector we found on the last page: \(\lambda_1 = 4 + 3i\) with \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We follow the three steps. Step 1 is quick: the complex-valued solution is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}e^{(4 + 3i)t}. \end{equation*}
    Step 2 is where the work happens, so we break it into two parts, with the details of each in a dropdown.
    🔗
    Solution 1. Details for Step 2a: Apply Euler’s formula and multiply out
    We apply Euler’s formula to the exponential:
    \begin{equation*} e^{(4 + 3i)t} = e^{4t}\left(\cos(3t) + i\sin(3t)\right), \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}\left(\cos(3t) + i\sin(3t)\right) = e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(3t) + i\sin(3t) \\ -i\cos(3t) - i^2\sin(3t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Using \(i^2 = -1\text{,}\) the second component becomes \(\sin(3t) - i\cos(3t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Step 2a leaves us with
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(3t) + i\sin(3t) \\ \sin(3t) - i\cos(3t) \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    which no longer has any \(i\) in the exponent, so we can separate the components.
    🔗
    Solution 2. Details for Step 2b: Separate into real and imaginary parts
    We separate everything with an \(i\) from everything without:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(3t) + i\sin(3t) \\ \sin(3t) - i\cos(3t) \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(3t) \\ \sin(3t) \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} + i\underbrace{e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \sin(3t) \\ -\cos(3t) \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{z}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Step 2b identifies our two real-valued solutions:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{y} = e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(3t) \\ \sin(3t) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{z} = e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \sin(3t) \\ -\cos(3t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Step 3: the general solution is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}(t) = c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z} = c_1 e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos(3t) \\ \sin(3t) \end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{4t}\begin{bmatrix} \sin(3t) \\ -\cos(3t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Notice there is no \(i\) anywhere in the final answer!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.5.4. Answers Can Look Very Different.

    We only used one eigenvalue-eigenvector pair, \(\lambda_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_1\text{;}\) the conjugate pair \(\lambda_2\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) is never needed (though using it instead also works). Because of this choice, and because eigenvectors themselves are only determined up to a multiple, correct answers for \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) can look quite different from each other. If your answer doesn’t match a friend’s, that alone doesn’t mean either of you is wrong!
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Euler’s formula removes \(i\) from the exponent: for \(\lambda = p + qi\text{,}\) we have \(e^{(p+qi)t} = e^{pt}(\cos(qt) + i\sin(qt))\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The three steps: write the complex solution \(\mathbf{v}_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\text{,}\) separate it into real and imaginary parts \(\mathbf{y} + i\mathbf{z}\) using Euler’s formula, and form the general solution \(\mathbf{x}(t) = c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Simplify with \(i^2 = -1\text{:}\) when multiplying out the components, terms with two factors of \(i\) become real.
      🔗
      🔗
    • No \(i\) in the final answer: the general solution \(c_1\mathbf{y} + c_2\mathbf{z}\) is entirely real-valued. There should be no \(i\) appearing in your final answer.
