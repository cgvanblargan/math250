    Section 3.5.1 A First Look at Non-Homogeneous Equations

    So far we have only solved 2nd order, constant-coefficient DEs where the right-hand side is zero:
    \begin{equation*} ay'' + by' + cy = 0. \end{equation*}
    These are called homogeneous equations. Now we turn to the non-homogeneous case, where the right-hand side is a nonzero function of \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} ay'' + by' + cy = g(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solving this in full generality is hard. But for certain simple, well-behaved choices of \(g(t)\text{,}\) we can make progress with a clever strategy (which we will call the method of undetermined coefficients). On this page we focus on functions of the form
    \begin{equation*} g(t) = a\cos(kt) + b\sin(kt), \end{equation*}
    and we will discover the method by working through examples, so treat this as an exploration for now. Over the next few pages, we will have fully laid out the strategy!
    🔗

    Exploration 3.5.1. Example 1: Finding a Particular Solution.

    We want to find a particular solution \(y_p\) to
    \begin{equation*} y'' + 4y = 10\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    That means: a single specific function \(y_p(t)\) that makes this equation true.
    🔗

    (a) What should we guess for \(y_p\text{?}\)

    We need \(y_p'' + 4y_p = 10\cos(3t)\text{.}\) The right-hand side is \(10\cos(3t)\text{,}\) so we need a function whose second derivative is also a multiple of \(\cos(3t)\text{.}\) Which of the following is the most natural guess? (Don’t worry, we haven’t learned a rule for this yet. Take your best guess!)
    🔗
    • \(y_p = A\cos(3t)\)
    • Yes! Since \(\frac{d^2}{dt^2}[\cos(3t)] = -9\cos(3t)\text{,}\) differentiating twice brings back a cosine, which is exactly what we need on the left to match \(10\cos(3t)\) on the right. We just have to find the right value of \(A\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    • \(y_p = Ae^{3t}\)
    • An exponential would give \(y_p'' = 9Ae^{3t}\text{,}\) so the left side becomes \(13Ae^{3t}\text{,}\) with no cosines anywhere. We can’t match \(10\cos(3t)\) this way.
    • \(y_p = At\)
    • A linear function gives \(y_p'' = 0\text{,}\) so the left side is just \(4At\text{,}\) a polynomial and not a cosine. This won’t work.
    • \(y_p = A\sin(3t)\)
    • Close in spirit! A sine would give \(y_p'' = -9A\sin(3t)\text{,}\) so the left side is \(-9A\sin(3t) + 4A\sin(3t) = -5A\sin(3t)\text{.}\) That’s a sine, but the right-hand side is a cosine, so they can’t be equal for any value of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (b) Plugging in and solving for \(A\).

    Now we plug the guess \(y_p = A\cos(3t)\) into \(y'' + 4y = 10\cos(3t)\) and solve for \(A\text{.}\) Computing derivatives:
    \begin{align*} y_p' \amp= -3A\sin(3t)\\ y_p'' \amp= -9A\cos(3t) \end{align*}
    Substituting:
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{-9A\cos(3t)}_{y''} + \underbrace{4A\cos(3t)}_{4y} = 10\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    Every term has a factor of \(\cos(3t)\text{,}\) so we divide through by it:
    \begin{equation*} -9A + 4A = 10 \implies -5A = 10 \implies A = -2. \end{equation*}
    A particular solution is:
    \begin{equation*} y_p = -2\cos(3t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So for the ODE \(y'' + 4y = 10\cos(3t)\text{,}\) we took the guess \(y_p = A\cos(3t)\text{.}\) Plugging it in gave us the equation \(-5A = 10\text{,}\) and solving gave \(A = -2\text{,}\) so the particular solution is \(y_p = -2\cos(3t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We now try a similar example, but this time the ODE includes a \(y'\) term. Let’s see what happens when we try the same type of guess.
    🔗

    Exploration 3.5.2. Example 2: When the Same Guess Doesn’t Work.

    Now try the same strategy on a different equation:
    \begin{equation*} 3y'' + 4y' - 2y = 2\cos(t). \end{equation*}
    Guess \(y_p = A\cos(t)\) and see what happens.
    🔗

    (a) Plugging in.

    With \(y_p = A\cos(t)\text{,}\) compute \(y_p'\) and \(y_p''\text{,}\) substitute into \(3y'' + 4y' - 2y = 2\cos(t)\text{,}\) and simplify as much as you can. What do you get?
    🔗
    Hint.
    You’ll get both \(\sin(t)\) and \(\cos(t)\) terms on the left side. Collect them separately.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(y_p = A\cos(t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} y_p' \amp= -A\sin(t)\\ y_p'' \amp= -A\cos(t) \end{align*}
    Substituting:
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{3(-A\cos(t))}_{3y''} + \underbrace{4(-A\sin(t))}_{4y'} + \underbrace{(-2)(A\cos(t))}_{-2y} = 2\cos(t). \end{equation*}
    Collecting like terms:
    \begin{equation*} -5A\cos(t) - 4A\sin(t) = 2\cos(t). \end{equation*}
    Rearranging:
    \begin{equation*} -4A\sin(t) = (2 + 5A)\cos(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) Why we’re stuck.

    We need \(-4A\sin(t) = (2 + 5A)\cos(t)\) to hold for every value of \(t\text{.}\) But a sine and a cosine can never be equal for all \(t\) unless both sides are zero. No matter what value we pick for \(A\text{,}\) the left side is a sine and the right side is a cosine, and there is no way to make them match. Our guess has failed!
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) What went wrong?

    After substituting \(y_p = A\cos(t)\) we ended up with a \(\sin(t)\) term stuck on the left side that couldn’t be cancelled. Why did this happen? (this is exploratory, so take your best shot!)
    🔗
    • The \(y'\) term in the ODE. Differentiating \(A\cos(t)\) gives \(-A\sin(t)\text{,}\) and that sine got multiplied by the coefficient of \(y'\text{,}\) leaving a sine term with no way to cancel it.
    • Exactly! In Example 1, there was no \(y'\) term, so differentiating \(A\cos(3t)\) produced a \(\sin(3t)\) that immediately disappeared again. Here the \(4y'\) term keeps that sine alive, and our guess has no sine to absorb it. \(\checkmark\)
    • The ODE has a \(y\) term with a negative coefficient (\(-2y\)).
    • The sign on \(y\) doesn’t cause the problem. Even with a positive coefficient, differentiating \(A\cos(t)\) would still produce a \(\sin(t)\) via the \(y'\) term.
    • The right-hand side is \(2\cos(t)\) instead of a higher frequency like \(10\cos(3t)\text{.}\)
    • The frequency of the right-hand side doesn’t cause the trouble here. The issue appears regardless of whether we have \(\cos(t)\) or \(\cos(3t)\) on the right.
    • We should have included a coefficient on the guess, like \(y_p = 2A\cos(t)\text{.}\)
    • The extra factor of 2 doesn’t help — it’s still just a constant times \(\cos(t)\text{,}\) and the \(y'\) term will still produce an uncancelled \(\sin(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    So the guess \(y_p = A\cos(t)\) failed because the \(y'\) term introduced a \(\sin(t)\) that had nowhere to go. We need a guess with enough flexibility to absorb both sine and cosine terms. Before moving on: can you think of how to modify the guess to fix this? We will work through this on the next page!
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • The method: To find a particular solution to a non-homogeneous equation, we can guess the form of \(y_p\text{,}\) plug it in, and solve for the unknown constants. Because the constants are initially unknown, this approach is called the method of undetermined coefficients.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The guess matters: As seen in Example 2, guessing \(y_p = A\cos(t)\) alone won’t always work. Differentiating a cosine produces a sine, and if the ODE has a \(y'\) term, that sine has nowhere to go. On the next page we’ll see what guess does always work for sinusoidal right-hand sides.
