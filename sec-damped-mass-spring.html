    Section 3.3.1 The Damped Mass-Spring System

    In Section 3.2.1, we modeled a mass attached to a spring and derived the ODE \(mx'' + kx = 0\text{.}\) That model assumed no friction whatsoever, so the mass oscillated forever. In reality, friction always acts to slow things down. We now add a dashpot to the system to model this frictional resistance.
    🔗
    A dashpot is a mechanical device that resists motion. The spring and dashpot both connect the mass to the wall, as shown in the animation below. Press Play to see how the dashpot changes the behavior compared to the undamped case.
    🔗
    Let’s spend a moment deriving the ODE for this system using a bit of physics. Once we have it, we can put all our solving techniques to use and dig into what the solutions actually look like. We’ll go through the derivation once here, then mostly just work with the ODE itself from here on.
    🔗
    As in Section 3.2.1, we measure position \(x(t)\) from the equilibrium position, with \(x > 0\) meaning the spring is stretched and \(x < 0\) meaning it is compressed. By Fact 3.2.2, the restoring force from the spring is:
    \begin{equation*} F_s = -kx, \end{equation*}
    where \(k > 0\) is the spring constant. The dashpot contributes a damping force proportional to velocity:
    \begin{equation*} F_R = -cx', \end{equation*}
    where \(c > 0\) is the damping constant. The negative sign means the dashpot always resists the direction of motion.
    🔗
    By Newton’s second law, the total force equals \(mx''\text{.}\) Since the only forces acting on the mass are \(F_s\) and \(F_R\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{equation*} mx'' = F_s + F_R = -kx - cx'. \end{equation*}
    Rearranging gives the damped mass-spring ODE:
    \begin{equation*} mx'' + cx' + kx = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.2. The Damped Mass-Spring System.

    The position \(x(t)\) of a mass \(m\) on a spring with constant \(k\) and damping constant \(c\) satisfies:
    \begin{equation*} mx'' + cx' + kx = 0, \end{equation*}
    where \(m, k, c > 0\text{.}\) This is called free damped motion: free because there is no external driving force, and damped because the dashpot dissipates energy.
    🔗
    🔗
    To solve \(mx'' + cx' + kx = 0\text{,}\) we use the same characteristic equation approach from Section 3.1.3. The characteristic equation is:
    \begin{equation*} mr^2 + cr + k = 0. \end{equation*}
    By the quadratic formula, the roots are:
    \begin{equation*} r = \frac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2 - 4km}}{2m}. \end{equation*}
    The expression \(D = c^2 - 4km\) is the discriminant, and its sign tells us which of the three cases from Theorem 3.1.1 we’re dealing with.
    🔗
    You’ve already seen how the sign of the discriminant determines the type of roots back in Exercise 3.1.7.7; let’s make sure that’s fresh in your mind.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.3.

    For the characteristic equation \(mr^2 + cr + k = 0\text{,}\) which condition on \(c\text{,}\) \(m\text{,}\) and \(k\) gives two distinct real roots?
    🔗
    Note that the discriminant is \(D=c^2-4km\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(c^2 - 4km > 0\)
    • Correct! When the discriminant is positive, the square root is real and nonzero, giving two distinct real roots \(r = \frac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2-4km}}{2m}\text{.}\) \(\checkmark\)
    • \(c^2 - 4km = 0\)
    • When the discriminant is zero the square root vanishes, giving one repeated root \(r = -c/(2m)\text{,}\) not two distinct roots.
    • \(c^2 - 4km < 0\)
    • When the discriminant is negative the square root is imaginary, giving complex conjugate roots, not two distinct real roots.
    • \(c^2 - 4km \geq 0\)
    • This includes the case \(c^2 - 4km = 0\text{,}\) which gives a repeated root, not two distinct roots. Strict inequality is needed.
    🔗
    As we saw in Theorem 3.1.1, each type of roots leads to a qualitatively different form of the general solution. In the mass-spring context, each case also has a physical name that describes how heavily the system is damped, depending on the size of the damping constant \(c\) relative to \(m\) and \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.4. The Three Damping Cases.

    For free damped motion \(mx'' + cx' + kx = 0\) with \(m, k, c > 0\text{,}\) the system falls into one of three cases depending on the discriminant \(D = c^2 - 4km\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • Overdamped (\(c^2 > 4km\)): two distinct real roots. The damping constant \(c\) is large relative to \(m\) and \(k\text{,}\) so the system is slowed down the most.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Critically damped (\(c^2 = 4km\)): one repeated real root. This is the borderline case: the exact value of \(c\) that separates overdamped from underdamped.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Underdamped (\(0 < c^2 < 4km\)): complex conjugate roots. The damping constant \(c\) is small, so the system still oscillates, just with decaying amplitude.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In all three cases \(c > 0\text{,}\) so the system is damped. When \(c = 0\) we recover the undamped system \(mx'' + kx = 0\) from Section 3.2.1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • The ODE: Adding a dashpot gives \(mx'' + cx' + kx = 0\text{,}\) called free damped motion. The new term \(cx'\) models friction proportional to velocity.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The discriminant: The sign of \(D = c^2 - 4km\) determines the type of roots of the characteristic equation and therefore the qualitative behavior of solutions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Three cases: Overdamped (\(c^2 > 4km\)), critically damped (\(c^2 = 4km\)), and underdamped (\(0 < c^2 < 4km\)). Larger \(c\) means more damping.
      🔗
      🔗
