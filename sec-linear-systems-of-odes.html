    Section 5.2.1 Linear Systems of Differential Equations

    Now that we have some basic understanding of matrices, we have the tools to study systems of ODEs! So far in this course, every differential equation has had just one unknown function. But many real-world situations involve several quantities that affect each other.
    🔗
    For example, suppose an island has a population of rabbits, \(R(t)\text{,}\) and a population of foxes, \(F(t)\text{.}\) The foxes eat the rabbits, so each population affects how the other grows. A (simplified) model might look like:
    \begin{align*} R' \amp= 2R - 3F\\ F' \amp= R - F \end{align*}
    Looking at this a bit closer, we see the \(2R\) is the rate in, namely that the rabbits breed proportional to the population (which was a common model we saw at the very beginning of these notes). But then the \(-3F\) is the rate out at which the foxes eat the rabbits. Meanwhile, \(F' = R - F\) says the fox population thrives when there are a lot of rabbits to eat (\(+R\)), but declines at rate equal to its population (\(-F\)), as larger fox populations create competition for food.
    🔗
    The catch is that we can’t just solve for \(R(t)\) by itself, because its equation involves \(F(t)\) (and vice versa!). The two equations are tangled together. This is a system of differential equations, and we need to solve both equations simultaneously. In this section we’ll set up the language for these systems, and over the rest of the chapter we’ll learn how to solve them in certain cases.
    🔗

    Definition 5.2.1. First-Order Linear System.

    A first-order linear system with two functions \(x_1(t)\) and \(x_2(t)\) can be written in the form:
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= p_{11}(t)x_1 + p_{12}(t)x_2 + f_1(t)\\ x_2' \amp= p_{21}(t)x_1 + p_{22}(t)x_2 + f_2(t) \end{align*}
    where \(x_1(t)\) and \(x_2(t)\) are functions that solve both ODEs simultaneously.
    🔗
    🔗
    Our rabbits and foxes are an example of a first-order linear system. Let’s rewrite the system and label each coefficient:
    \begin{align*} R' \amp= \overbrace{2}^{p_{11}(t)}R + \overbrace{(-3)}^{p_{12}(t)}F\\ F' \amp= \underbrace{1}_{p_{21}(t)}R + \underbrace{(-1)}_{p_{22}(t)}F \end{align*}
    That is, \(p_{11}(t) = 2\text{,}\) \(p_{12}(t) = -3\text{,}\) \(p_{21}(t) = 1\text{,}\) and \(p_{22}(t) = -1\text{.}\) Here all the \(p\)’s are constants, and there are no leftover terms, so \(f_1(t) = f_2(t) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here is an example where the coefficients are functions of \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= \overbrace{\sin(t)}^{p_{11}(t)}x_1 + \overbrace{3t^2}^{p_{12}(t)} x_2 + \overbrace{e^{3t}}^{f_1(t)}\\ x_2' \amp= \underbrace{(-2)}_{p_{21}(t)}x_1 + \underbrace{t}_{p_{22}(t)} x_2 + \underbrace{5}_{f_2(t)} \end{align*}
    So \(p_{11}(t) = \sin(t)\text{,}\) \(p_{12}(t) = 3t^2\text{,}\) \(p_{21}(t) = -2\text{,}\) \(p_{22}(t) = t\text{,}\) \(f_1(t) = e^{3t}\text{,}\) and \(f_2(t) = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We collect the coefficients into a matrix and the functions into vectors:
    🔗
    • The coefficient matrix is \(\mathbf{P}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} p_{11}(t) \amp p_{12}(t) \\ p_{21}(t) \amp p_{22}(t) \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • And \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{f}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} f_1(t) \\ f_2(t) \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Continuing our example from above: the system
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= \sin(t)x_1 + 3t^2 x_2 + e^{3t}\\ x_2' \amp= -2x_1 + t x_2 + 5 \end{align*}
    has coefficient matrix and vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{P}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \sin(t) \amp 3t^2 \\ -2 \amp t \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{f}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{3t} \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Notice each entry \(p_{ij}(t)\) of the coefficient matrix is the coefficient of \(x_j\) in the equation for \(x_i'\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{f}(t)\) collects the leftover terms that don’t involve \(x_1\) or \(x_2\) at all.
    🔗

    Fact 5.2.2. Matrix Form of a Linear System.

    The system above can be written as a matrix equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{f}(t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Why are these the same? Let’s expand out the right-hand side using matrix-vector multiplication and addition:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{f}(t) \amp= \begin{bmatrix} p_{11}(t) \amp p_{12}(t) \\ p_{21}(t) \amp p_{22}(t) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} f_1(t) \\ f_2(t) \end{bmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{bmatrix} p_{11}x_1 + p_{12}x_2 + f_1(t) \\ p_{21}x_1 + p_{22}x_2 + f_2(t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    The left-hand side is \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1' \\ x_2' \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Setting the two sides equal component-by-component recovers exactly the two equations of our system! So the matrix equation is just a compact way to package the whole system at once.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.3. Writing a System as a Matrix Equation.

    Write the system:
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= \sin(t)x_1 - x_2 + 5\\ x_2' \amp= e^t x_1 + 6t \end{align*}
    as a matrix equation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We need to be careful to match each coefficient to its correct function, so that it lands in the correct spot in the matrix. To make this clear, let’s rewrite the system with every coefficient written out explicitly, including the ones that are \(0\) or \(-1\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= \sin(t)x_1 + (-1)x_2 + 5\\ x_2' \amp= e^t x_1 + 0 x_2 + 6t \end{align*}
    Now every equation has an \(x_1\) term, an \(x_2\) term, and a leftover term, so we can just read off the entries:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} \sin(t) \amp -1 \\ e^t \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6t \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s end with you trying one real quick!
    🔗

    Activity 5.2.1. Matrix Form Practice.

    Which matrix equation represents the system below?
    \begin{align*} x_1' \amp= t^2 x_1 + 7\\ x_2' \amp= -x_1 + \cos(t) x_2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    • \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} t^2 \amp 0 \\ -1 \amp \cos(t) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Correct! The first equation has no \(x_2\) term, so \(p_{12}(t) = 0\text{,}\) and the second equation has no leftover term, so \(f_2(t) = 0\text{.}\) The \(7\) doesn’t involve \(x_1\) or \(x_2\text{,}\) so it belongs in \(\mathbf{f}(t)\text{,}\) not the matrix.
    • \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} t^2 \amp 7 \\ -1 \amp \cos(t) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Careful with the \(7\text{!}\) The entry \(p_{12}(t)\) is the coefficient of \(x_2\) in the first equation, but the \(7\) is not multiplied by \(x_2\text{:}\) it’s a leftover term with no \(x_1\) or \(x_2\) attached. It belongs in the vector \(\mathbf{f}(t)\text{,}\) and \(p_{12}(t) = 0\text{.}\)
    • \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} t^2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp \cos(t) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • So close! Check the sign on \(p_{21}(t)\text{:}\) the second equation reads \(-x_1\text{,}\) so the coefficient is \(-1\text{,}\) not \(1\text{.}\)
    • \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} t^2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp \cos(t) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
    • Rows first! The \(-1\) is the coefficient of \(x_1\) in the second equation, so it goes in row \(2\text{,}\) column \(1\text{:}\) that is, \(p_{21}(t) = -1\text{.}\) Here it’s been placed in row \(1\text{,}\) column \(2\) instead.
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • A system of ODEs has multiple unknown functions whose equations are tangled together, so we must solve them simultaneously.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A first-order linear system is built from coefficients \(p_{ij}(t)\) and leftover terms \(f_i(t)\text{,}\) where \(p_{ij}(t)\) is the coefficient of \(x_j\) in the equation for \(x_i'\) (rows first!).
      🔗
      🔗
    • Any such system can be packaged as the matrix equation \(\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{f}(t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When converting a system to matrix form, be careful to line up the coefficients correctly (some will be \(0\) if a term doesn’t explicitly show up in the system).
