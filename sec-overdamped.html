    Section 3.3.3 Behavior of Overdamped Solutions

    Now let’s swing to the other extreme. When \(c^2 > 4km\text{,}\) the damping is so strong that the characteristic equation has two distinct real roots \(r_1\) and \(r_2\text{,}\) and the system is called overdamped. The general solution takes the form:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}. \end{equation*}
    If you want to see why both roots are negative, check out the dropdown below.
    🔗

    Why are both roots negative? (can skim).

    By the quadratic formula, the roots of \(mr^2+cr+k=0\) are
    \begin{equation*} r = \frac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2-4km}}{2m}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(c^2 > 4km\text{,}\) the quantity under the square root is positive, but it is also smaller than \(c^2\text{,}\) so \(\sqrt{c^2-4km} \lt c\text{.}\) That means both \(-c + \sqrt{c^2-4km}\) and \(-c - \sqrt{c^2-4km}\) are negative numbers. Dividing by \(2m > 0\) keeps them negative, so both \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are negative.
    🔗
    🔗

    Observation 3.3.1. Overdamped Solution Form.

    When \(c^2 > 4km\text{,}\) the characteristic equation has two distinct real roots \(r_1\) and \(r_2\text{,}\) and the system is overdamped. Since \(m, c, k > 0\text{,}\) both roots are negative. The general solution takes the form:
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}, \end{equation*}
    where \(r_1 \lt 0\) and \(r_2 \lt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Unlike the underdamped case in Section 3.3.2, there are no trig functions anywhere in sight. The solution is just a sum of two decaying exponentials, with no oscillation at all.
    🔗
    Take a look at the animation below to see how quickly the mass slows down in this case!
    🔗
    In the animation above, the mass crossed equilibrium exactly once on its way to rest. But is that guaranteed every time, or does it depend on the values of \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{?}\) Play around with the explorer below and see what you notice.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3.4.

    Based on your exploration above, what are the possibilities for how many times the mass passes through the equilibrium position \(x = 0\) in an overdamped system?
    🔗
    • Never or exactly once.
    • Correct! The mass passes through equilibrium at most once. Depending on the values of \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{,}\) it may cross once or never cross at all. \(\checkmark\)
    • Exactly once, always.
    • Not always. Try \(c_1 = 2\) and \(c_2 = 1\) in the explorer above. The curve never crosses \(x = 0\text{.}\)
    • Infinitely many times.
    • That is the underdamped case. In an overdamped system there is no oscillation, so the curve cannot cross equilibrium infinitely many times.
    • Exactly twice.
    • Try as many values of \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) as you like in the explorer. You will not find a case where the curve crosses twice.
    🔗
    The explorer suggests the mass crosses equilibrium at most once, no matter what \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are. Let’s confirm this by digging into the algebra.
    🔗

    Example 3.3.5. At Most One Equilibrium Crossing.

    Show that the mass in an overdamped system passes through the equilibrium position at most one time.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We want to find all times \(t \geq 0\) where \(x(t) = 0\text{.}\) Setting the general solution equal to zero:
    \begin{equation*} c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t} = 0. \end{equation*}
    Since \(e^{r_2 t} \neq 0\) for all \(t\text{,}\) we can divide both sides by \(e^{r_2 t}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} c_1 e^{(r_1 - r_2)t} + c_2 = 0, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} e^{(r_1 - r_2)t} = -\frac{c_2}{c_1}. \end{equation*}
    Taking the natural log of both sides (when the right side is positive):
    \begin{equation*} t = \frac{1}{r_1 - r_2} \ln\!\left(-\frac{c_2}{c_1}\right). \end{equation*}
    This is at most one value of \(t\text{.}\) It gives a valid crossing only when \(-c_2/c_1 > 0\) and the resulting \(t \geq 0\text{.}\) Otherwise the mass never crosses equilibrium at all.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So at most one crossing along the way, but where does the mass end up after all that?
    🔗

    Observation 3.3.6.

    Since both \(r_1 \lt 0\) and \(r_2 \lt 0\text{,}\) both exponential terms decay to zero:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} \Bigl( \underbrace{c_1 e^{r_1 t}}_{\to\, 0} + \underbrace{c_2 e^{r_2 t}}_{\to\, 0} \Bigr) = 0. \end{equation*}
    The mass always approaches equilibrium in the long run, crossing it at most once on the way there.
    🔗
    🔗

    Key Takeaways.

    • Solution form: Overdamped solutions have the form \(x(t) = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}\) where \(r_1, r_2 \lt 0\) are two distinct real roots.
      🔗
      🔗
    • No oscillation: The solution is a sum of two decaying exponentials with no trigonometric part, so there is no oscillation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Equilibrium crossings: The mass passes through \(x = 0\) at most once. Depending on \(c_1\) and \(c_2\text{,}\) it may cross once or never.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Long-term behavior: \(\displaystyle\lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = 0\text{.}\)
